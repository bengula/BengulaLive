For the first few years after the Data Protection Act (DPA) was passed in 2019, most Kenyan SMEs ignored it, assuming it was a problem for Safaricom, the banks, and digital lenders. 

That assumption is now a material financial risk. The Office of the Data Protection Commissioner (ODPC) has ramped up enforcement, and their administrative penalties (up to KES 5 million or 1% of annual turnover, whichever is lower) are large enough to wipe out a small business. If you run a Shopify store, a clinic, a school, or even a hardware shop that collects phone numbers for M-Pesa receipts, you are governed by this Act.

This guide is for the SME owner. It strips away the legal theory and focuses on the operational requirements: who has to register, what you actually have to do, and how to stop treating customer data as a shared public resource.

> **Key Insight:** The most common ODPC complaint against SMEs is not about complex cyber-attacks or hacked servers. It is about **unsolicited marketing** (sending promotional SMS messages to people who did not ask for them) and **debt shaming** (contacting a customer's relatives without consent). Both are entirely preventable by fixing your consent process.

### Part 1: Are You a Data Controller or a Data Processor?

The DPA divides businesses into two categories:

- **Data Controller:** You decide *why* and *how* personal data is processed. If you own an e-commerce store and collect customer names and addresses to deliver shoes, you are the controller.
- **Data Processor:** You process data on behalf of a controller. If you run a bulk SMS platform that businesses use to send texts, you are the processor.

Many SMEs are controllers. If you decide to collect the data for your own business purposes, you carry the primary responsibility for protecting it and ensuring you have the legal right to use it.

### Part 2: Do You Have to Register with the ODPC?

Not every SME must register with the ODPC, but the net is wider than most realize. The Data Protection (Registration of Data Controllers and Data Processors) Regulations, 2021, set the thresholds.

**Mandatory Registration applies if:**
1. Your annual turnover is above **KES 5 million** AND you have more than **10 employees**.
2. **OR**, regardless of size or turnover, you process personal data for specific high-risk activities.

The "regardless of size" exemptions catch many small businesses. You **must** register, even if you are a sole proprietor, if you operate in sectors including:
- Healthcare (clinics, pharmacies)
- Education (schools, daycares)
- Property management (agencies collecting tenant IDs)
- Financial services (including micro-lending and SACCOs)
- Direct marketing (if marketing is your core business)
- Hospitality (hotels collecting guest IDs)

Registration is done online via the ODPC portal and involves a fee (typically KES 4,000 for micro and small enterprises, valid for two years). Operating while unregistered when the regulations require you to be is an offence.

### Part 3: The Reality of "Consent"

The foundation of data protection is lawful processing. You must have a legal basis for collecting and using a customer's data. For SMEs, the most common legal bases are:
1. **Performance of a contract:** You need their address to deliver the goods they bought.
2. **Consent:** They explicitly agreed you could use their data for a specific purpose (like marketing).

**The Marketing Trap:**
This is where SMEs fail. Collecting a phone number to send an M-Pesa payment receipt (performance of a contract) **does not** give you the right to send that number promotional bulk SMS messages every Friday. 

Under the DPA, consent must be express, unequivocal, free, specific, and informed. Pre-ticked boxes on a website checkout ("Subscribe to our newsletter") are not valid consent. Buying a list of phone numbers from a vendor and texting them is a direct violation. 

If you want to market to your customers, you must ask them: "Can we send you offers?" and they must say yes. Furthermore, every marketing message must include a clear, working opt-out mechanism.

### Part 4: The Core SME Compliance Checklist

Compliance doesn't require a dedicated legal department. It requires operational discipline.

1. **Audit Your Data:** What personal data do you collect? (IDs, phone numbers, emails, health records). Where is it stored? (A spreadsheet? A cloud CRM? A physical notebook?). Who has access to it?
2. **Write a Privacy Policy:** You must inform customers what data you collect, why you collect it, how long you keep it, and their rights (including the right to request deletion). This must be available on your website or at your premises.
3. **Fix Your Forms:** Ensure any form (online or paper) that collects data clearly states the purpose and includes a specific opt-in check box if you intend to use the data for marketing.
4. **Secure the Data:** If your customer list is on a laptop, that laptop must have a password. If it is in a physical notebook, the notebook should be in a locked drawer, not sitting on the reception desk for anyone to photograph.
5. **Report Breaches:** If personal data is lost, stolen, or accessed unlawfully, you have a statutory duty to notify the ODPC within 72 hours if the breach poses a risk to the rights and freedoms of the individuals.

### Part 5: The Cost of Getting It Wrong

The ODPC is not a paper tiger. Under section 63 of the Act, the Commissioner may serve a penalty notice requiring a person to pay an administrative fine of up to **KES 5,000,000 or 1% of the undertaking's annual turnover**, whichever is lower.

In recent enforcement actions, the ODPC has fined:
- Educational institutions for posting pictures of minors without parental consent.
- Digital lenders for calling third-party contacts without authorization.
- Hospitality venues for misusing guest register data.

Beyond the regulatory fine, a data breach destroys trust. A customer who realizes you sold their phone number to a spam marketer, or that your unsecured Google Sheet leaked their purchase history, is a customer who will not return.

### Bengula View

Data protection is ultimately about data governance, which is a core business asset. An SME that knows exactly what data it holds, why it holds it, and has explicit permission to use it is an SME that can run highly effective, targeted digital marketing campaigns (as discussed in [Digital Growth for SMEs](/blog/digital-growth-kenya-sme-guide)). 

Conversely, an SME that harvests numbers indiscriminately and blasts untargeted SMS messages is not only breaking the law, it is wasting its marketing budget. Treat customer data with the same security and respect you treat customer cash, and compliance becomes a byproduct of good business practice.
