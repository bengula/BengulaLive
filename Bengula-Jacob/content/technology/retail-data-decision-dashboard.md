### The Problem: Decisions Arrived After the Damage

A multi-branch retailer operating between Mombasa and Nairobi was making buying, pricing, and stock-transfer decisions from stale spreadsheets. Reports arrived almost thirty days late. By the time the owner saw that a line was underpriced or dead stock was piling up, the cash had already been trapped.

The business did not need a complex enterprise system. It needed a reliable daily view of sales, stock, and margin.

### What the Dashboard Had to Show

The dashboard was built around decisions, not decoration. Every view answered one commercial question:

- **What sold today, and at what margin?**
- **Which branch is overstocked or understocked?**
- **Which products are tying up cash without moving?**
- **Which price changes improved margin without hurting volume?**
- **Which supplier orders should be delayed, reduced, or accelerated?**

The aim was to move the owner from monthly guesswork to weekly, evidence-led action.

### The Build: Small Stack, Fast Payoff

The temptation in projects like this is to buy a heavyweight system and spend a year implementing it. We did the opposite, and the sequence matters more than the software:

1. **Clean the source data first.** The point-of-sale exports had duplicate product codes and inconsistent branch naming. Until those were fixed, no dashboard could be trusted. This unglamorous step took a third of the project.
2. **Automate the flow, not the analysis.** Daily sales and stock exports were piped into one cloud workbook automatically, so nobody had to remember to update anything. A dashboard that depends on a diligent human is a dashboard that dies in month two.
3. **One screen, five questions.** The owner's view was deliberately limited to the five commercial questions above. Extra charts were refused. Clutter is how dashboards stop being read.
4. **A weekly decision meeting.** Every Monday, thirty minutes, same agenda: what moved, what stalled, what gets repriced, what gets transferred between branches. The dashboard existed to feed this meeting, not to look impressive.

The same "measure it before you scale it" discipline drove the online channel in [E-commerce Storefront & Conversion Launch](/blog/ecommerce-storefront-conversion-launch); the two projects share one philosophy applied to two sales channels.

### The Numbers That Changed

| Metric | Before | After |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Report lag | 30 days | Live / same day |
| Dead stock | Baseline | -38% in Q1 |
| Gross margin | Baseline | +5.2 percentage points |
| Pricing reviews | Occasional | Weekly |

The biggest win was cash release. Dead stock is not just an inventory problem; it is trapped working capital. Reducing it gave the business more room to buy fast-moving lines without relying on expensive short-term borrowing.

### Why Margin Visibility Matters

Retailers often know revenue but not contribution margin. That is dangerous. A product can sell quickly and still weaken the business if discounts, shrinkage, supplier terms, and logistics costs erase the profit.

The dashboard separated products into practical groups:

- **Winners:** high velocity and healthy margin.
- **Traffic builders:** high velocity but low margin, useful only when deliberately managed.
- **Cash traps:** slow movement and weak margin.
- **Pricing candidates:** products where demand stayed strong after small price adjustments.

This mirrors the discipline in [SME Packager Margin Optimization](/blog/sme-packager-optimization): growth is only good when unit economics are visible.

### The Banking Angle

Better retail data also improves financing conversations. A bank or supplier is more likely to support working capital when the business can show stock movement, margin, and cash conversion clearly. Without that evidence, the facility looks like a general overdraft. With it, the financing can be linked to fast-moving stock or receivables.

For the finance side of that conversation, [How Kenyan Banks Price Your Loan](/blog/how-kenyan-banks-price-loans) explains why better visibility can reduce perceived risk, and [What Is Accounts Receivable](/blog/what-is-accounts-receivable) explains how cash tied up in customer payments should be managed.

### Decision Framework: Is Your Reporting Costing You Money?

Answer these five honestly. Two or more "no" answers means the reporting gap is already taxing the business:

1. Can you state yesterday's sales and gross margin, by branch, without calling anyone?
2. Do you know which ten products tie up the most cash relative to how fast they move?
3. When did you last change a price based on data rather than a supplier's price change?
4. Could you show a lender three months of stock-turn and margin evidence this week?
5. Does anyone review the numbers on a fixed weekly rhythm, with decisions minuted?

### Bengula View

Retailers rarely have a data problem; they have a latency problem. The information exists in the till and the stock book, it just arrives too late to act on. The desk's view is that a modest, disciplined dashboard read weekly beats a sophisticated one read occasionally, and that the first money any multi-branch retailer spends on "digital" should go to seeing margin daily, not to a new website. Visibility funds everything else.
