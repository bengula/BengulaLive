Most Kenyan SMEs choose premises the way people choose a house: by location, size, and what the monthly rent feels like against what they think they can pay. Then they spend the next three years trying to sell their way out of a decision that was made in an afternoon.

Rent is not a feeling. It is a fixed cost that arrives on the first of the month whether the month was good or bad, it grows every year by contract, and it is the single largest controllable decision a retail or service business makes before it sells anything. The right question is not "can I afford KES 120,000 a month?" It is "what level of sales does this space demand, and is that level realistic here?"

This article gives you the tools to answer that question before you sign: the occupancy cost ratio, the break-even sales a lease implies, the true cost of escalation and deposits, and the legal line at five years that decides whether a dispute with your landlord goes to a specialist tribunal or to the courts.

> **Key Insight:** Rent is paid out of gross profit, not out of sales. A shop on a 25% gross margin that pays 8% of its sales in occupancy costs is handing almost a third of its gross profit to its landlord before it pays a single salary. That is why the same rent that is comfortable for a pharmacy can be fatal for a grocer next door. Work out the sales your space requires, at your margin, before you look at the view from the window.

### Part 1: The Occupancy Cost Ratio

The occupancy cost ratio measures how much of every shilling of sales goes to the space you trade from.

$$
\text{Occupancy cost ratio} = \frac{\text{Base rent} + \text{Service charge} + \text{Other occupancy costs}}{\text{Net sales}} \times 100
$$

"Other occupancy costs" means anything you pay because you occupy that space: property rates or insurance the lease passes on to you, a shared security levy, and VAT on rent if your landlord is VAT-registered and you cannot claim it back. Electricity and water usually belong in operating costs, not occupancy, but be consistent.

**What is a healthy ratio?** There is no Kenyan official benchmark, and we will not invent one. The ranges below are working rules of thumb drawn from international retail practice and our own observation of Kenyan SME accounts. Treat them as a sense-check, not a standard.

| Business type | Typical gross margin | Occupancy cost ratio that usually works |
|---|---|---|
| Grocery, minimart, wholesale | Low (roughly 10% to 25%) | Low single digits, often under 5% |
| General retail, hardware, electronics | Moderate (roughly 25% to 40%) | Around 5% to 10% |
| Restaurants and cafés | High on food, offset by labour | Around 6% to 10% |
| Pharmacies, salons, specialist retail | Higher (roughly 35% to 55%) | Up to 10% to 15% |
| Professional offices | Service margin, high | Measured against fee income; often 5% to 12% |

**Confidence note:** These ranges are expert judgment, not measured Kenyan data. The principle behind them is documented and universal: the lower your gross margin, the lower the share of sales you can spend on space.

The better test is the one that uses your own margin.

$$
\text{Rent as a share of gross profit} = \frac{\text{Occupancy cost ratio}}{\text{Gross margin}}
$$

A business with an 8% occupancy ratio and a 25% gross margin spends 32% of its gross profit on space. The same 8% at a 50% margin is 16%. The first business has very little left for wages, stock financing and profit. The second has room to breathe.

### Part 2: The Sales a Lease Demands

Turn the ratio round and it tells you the sales a space requires before you sign.

$$
\text{Required monthly sales} = \frac{\text{Monthly occupancy cost}}{\text{Target occupancy ratio}}
$$

**Worked example.** A hardware retailer is offered a ground-floor unit at KES 90,000 a month, plus a service charge of KES 15,000, plus 16% VAT on both because the landlord is VAT-registered and the retailer is not. Monthly occupancy cost is therefore KES 121,800. The retailer's gross margin is 28%, and it targets an occupancy ratio of 8%.

- Required monthly sales: 121,800 ÷ 0.08 = **KES 1,522,500**
- That is about **KES 50,750 a day** over 30 trading days.
- At a 28% margin, those sales produce gross profit of KES 426,300, of which occupancy takes 121,800, or **29%**.

Now the decision is concrete. Does this street deliver KES 50,000 a day to a hardware shop? The current shop does KES 900,000 a month. If the move lifts sales by a third, to KES 1.2 million, the occupancy ratio becomes 10.2%, and the landlord takes 36% of gross profit. That might still work, but the owner now knows it is a bet on footfall, not a lease decision.

**The break-even view.** Rent is one fixed cost among several. Add salaries, security, insurance and loan repayments, and the full break-even is:

$$
\text{Break-even monthly sales} = \frac{\text{All fixed monthly costs}}{\text{Gross margin}}
$$

Our [SME financial ratios piece](/blog/sme-financial-ratios-kenya) covers margins and break-even in more depth. The point here is to run both numbers on every unit you consider, and to plan around the sales you can defend, not the sales you hope for.

### Part 3: Escalation Is a Compounding Cost

Most Kenyan commercial leases carry an annual rent escalation, commonly somewhere between 5% and 10% a year in our observation, written into the lease. It looks small. It compounds.

| Year | Rent at 5% escalation | Rent at 10% escalation |
|---|---:|---:|
| 1 | KES 90,000 | KES 90,000 |
| 2 | KES 94,500 | KES 99,000 |
| 3 | KES 99,225 | KES 108,900 |
| 4 | KES 104,186 | KES 119,790 |
| 5 | KES 109,396 | KES 131,769 |
| 6 | KES 114,865 | KES 144,946 |

At 10% a year, the rent in year six is 61% higher than in year one. Unless your sales grow at the same pace, your occupancy ratio climbs every year without anything changing in your shop. With inflation at, say, 5%, a 10% escalation is a real increase of roughly 5% a year, every year. Our piece on [inflation and real returns](/blog/inflation-shilling-real-return-kenya) explains the arithmetic.

**What to negotiate.** Ask for a lower fixed escalation, an escalation linked to inflation, a cap, or a rent-free first year that is not followed by an immediate jump. A landlord who refuses all four is telling you something about how they see the relationship.

### Part 4: Deposits, Advance Rent and Goodwill

Before you trade, a new lease usually asks for money that does not earn anything.

- **Security deposit.** Commonly one to three months' rent in our observation, held against damage or unpaid rent and refundable at the end of the lease, usually without interest.
- **Advance rent.** Often one to three months, or a quarter, paid in advance.
- **Goodwill.** In high-footfall streets, an outgoing tenant or a landlord may demand a lump sum for the right to the space. It is market practice rather than a legal requirement, it is usually non-refundable, and its value rests entirely on the footfall continuing.
- **Fit-out.** Shelving, partitions, signage, electrics and branding, which belong to the building when you leave unless the lease says otherwise.
- **Legal costs and stamp duty.** Stamp duty is charged on leases under the Stamp Duty Act, Cap 480, and secondary sources consistently describe the rate as 1% for leases of up to three years and 2% for longer leases, calculated on the rent. Confirm the current schedule and base with your advocate before you budget.

**Worked example.** Using the hardware unit above: a three-month deposit (KES 315,000 on rent and service charge before VAT), three months' advance rent and service charge with VAT (KES 365,400), and a fit-out of KES 400,000 come to **KES 1,080,400** before any goodwill. That is cash that would otherwise have bought stock. At a 28% margin, replacing it from trading needs about KES 3.9 million of sales.

Treat the up-front package as part of the price of the space. Compare units on the total cost over the lease, not on the monthly headline.

### Part 5: The Five-Year Line

Kenyan commercial tenancies sit on one side or the other of a line drawn by the **Landlord and Tenant (Shops, Hotels and Catering Establishments) Act, Cap 301**.

A **controlled tenancy** under section 2 is a tenancy of a shop, hotel or catering establishment that has not been put in writing, or that has been put in writing and is for a period **not exceeding five years**. Controlled tenancies carry statutory protection:

- **No termination or adverse change without notice in the prescribed form (section 4).** A landlord who wants to end the tenancy, raise the rent or change the terms against the tenant must serve a tenancy notice in the prescribed form.
- **At least two months' notice (section 4(4)).** A tenancy notice cannot take effect earlier than two months after the receiving party gets it, unless the tenancy provides for a longer period or the parties agree in writing to a shorter one.
- **Grounds and a reply window (section 4(5)).** The notice must state its grounds and require the tenant to say in writing, within one month, whether they agree to comply.
- **The Business Premises Rent Tribunal.** A tenant who does not accept a notice can refer it to the Tribunal before it takes effect, and the Tribunal has power to assess rent and decide disputes in controlled tenancies. The Tribunal has repeatedly struck down notices that were not in the prescribed form or gave less than two months.

A tenancy for **more than five years** in writing is outside the Act. Disputes go to the ordinary courts under the lease's terms and general contract and land law, which is slower and more expensive for a small tenant.

**Why this matters when you sign.** It is a common market observation that many Kenyan commercial leases are drafted for a term just over five years, such as five years and three months, which places them outside the controlled-tenancy regime. That is not illegal. But it means a tenant who signs one is giving up the two-month notice protection and access to the Tribunal in exchange for a longer term. Know which side of the line your lease falls on, and price that in.

Longer leases are also generally registrable against the title under the Land Registration Act. Registration protects a tenant if the building is sold. Ask your advocate whether your lease should be registered.

### Part 6: Clauses That Cost Money

Beyond the rent, these are the clauses that most often surprise SME tenants.

| Clause | What to check |
|---|---|
| Service charge | What it covers, how it is calculated, whether it can be increased, and whether you can see the accounts |
| Escalation | Rate, cap, and whether it applies to service charge as well |
| Repairs | Which repairs are yours, which are the landlord's, and the condition you must hand back in |
| Use clause | Whether you can change or widen what you sell without consent |
| Assignment and subletting | Whether you can sell the business with the lease, and on what terms |
| Break clause | Whether you can leave early, with what notice, and at what cost |
| Rent-free period | How long, and whether it covers service charge |
| Deposit refund | Timeline and the deductions the landlord may make |
| VAT | Whether rent is quoted inclusive or exclusive of VAT |

A **break clause** is often the most valuable clause an SME can negotiate. It turns a five-year commitment into an option, and it caps the downside of a location that does not work.

> **Risk Factors:** The most severe risk is a lease priced on hoped-for sales, which locks a business into a fixed cost it cannot meet once trading settles at a realistic level. The most common risk is escalation outpacing sales growth, which raises the occupancy ratio every year without any change in the shop. Up-front deposits, advance rent and goodwill drain working capital at the moment a new location most needs stock. And a tenant who signs a lease of just over five years without realising it loses the statutory notice protection and access to the Business Premises Rent Tribunal. A landlord who sells the building is a further risk for an unregistered long lease.

### Bengula View

From the credit desk, a lease is one of the first things we look at when an SME's cash flow stops making sense. The pattern repeats: a good business moves to a better street on optimistic sales, signs a long lease with 10% escalation, spends its working capital on deposits and fit-out, and then borrows to fund the stock it no longer has cash for. The overdraft becomes permanent and the lease is the reason.

We would weigh three things most heavily. First, decide on the sales the space demands at your real margin, not on the rent alone. Second, protect your working capital: a slightly worse location with a smaller up-front package often beats a prime unit that leaves you short of stock. Third, buy flexibility. A break clause, a shorter first term, or an escalation cap is worth more to a growing SME than a marginally lower headline rent.

When a lease is already signed and too expensive, the occupancy ratio is also the most persuasive number to take to a landlord. A tenant who can show their sales, their margin, and what the escalation is doing to their ratio is negotiating with data, not asking for a favour.

### Conclusion

Rent is a sales target in disguise. Calculate the occupancy cost ratio, turn it into the daily sales your space requires, run the escalation to the end of the term, and count the cash the lease absorbs before you trade. Then read the lease to see which side of the five-year line you are on. A space you can afford at realistic sales is a platform. A space you can only afford at hoped-for sales is a liability with a nice frontage.

### Frequently Asked Questions

**What percentage of sales should rent be?** It depends on your gross margin. Low-margin businesses such as groceries need occupancy costs in low single digits. Higher-margin businesses can carry 10% or more. Test it against gross profit, not just sales.

**Can my landlord raise the rent whenever they want?** In a controlled tenancy, a landlord who wants to raise rent against the lease terms must serve a notice in the prescribed form, with at least two months' notice, and you can refer it to the Business Premises Rent Tribunal. In a lease of more than five years, the lease terms govern.

**Is goodwill refundable?** Usually not. It is a market payment for access to a location, not a deposit. Get any goodwill arrangement in writing and treat it as a cost you may not recover.

**Should I sign a lease of five years and three months?** It gives you a longer secured term but takes you outside the controlled-tenancy regime and the Tribunal. Weigh that trade-off consciously, and negotiate a break clause if you sign.

**Does my business pay VAT on rent?** If your landlord is VAT-registered, commercial rent attracts VAT at 16%. If your business is VAT-registered, you can generally claim it as input tax; our [VAT for SMEs piece](/blog/vat-for-smes-kenya) explains the mechanics.
