A great many Kenyan businesses are owned by people who cannot stand in them. The salaried professional with a shop in their home town. The owner of a second branch across the county. The diaspora investor with a pharmacy run by a cousin. The farmer whose agrovet opens while they are in the field. In each case, the person at the counter decides what gets recorded, and the owner finds out what happened from whatever that person chooses to report.

Most of these businesses do not fail through dramatic theft. They bleed. A sale not rung up here, a discount given to a friend there, a little stock that walks out at closing, an expense paid from the till without a receipt. Each leak is small enough to explain. Together they are often the difference between a business that pays its owner and one that the owner keeps topping up.

This article is about the controls that let you run a business you are not standing in. It does not repeat what our other pieces own. The [13-week cash forecast](/blog/13-week-cash-forecast-kenya-sme) tells you what cash you will need, and the [M-Pesa for business piece](/blog/mpesa-for-business-paybill-till-kenya) sets up collection so money lands where it should. This piece is about the gap between the two: making sure that what happens at the counter is what reaches your books.

> **Key Insight:** Controls are not about distrusting your staff. They are about making the honest version of events the easiest one to record and the dishonest one the hardest to hide. The single most revealing test costs nothing: compare average daily sales on days you are present with days you are away. If the business consistently sells more when you are standing in it, the difference is the size of your problem, measured in shillings.

### Part 1: How Small Businesses Actually Leak

Leakage in a small shop rarely looks like theft from the outside. It looks like ordinary trading. These are the patterns we see most often in SME accounts and in conversations with owners.

| Leak | How it happens | What it looks like in the numbers |
|---|---|---|
| Unrecorded sales | Cash sale taken without being rung up or written down | Sales lower than footfall and stock movement suggest |
| Personal till or number | Customer paid to an attendant's own M-Pesa number | Digital sales fall while cash sales stay level |
| Unauthorised discounts | Price cut for friends or in exchange for a kickback | Gross margin drifts down without a cost explanation |
| Voids and refunds | A completed sale cancelled after the customer leaves, cash kept | Rising voids or refunds, clustered by person or time of day |
| Stock walking out | Goods taken at closing or passed to an accomplice | Stock count below book stock |
| Supplier collusion | Short deliveries signed for as full, or inflated invoices | Purchases rising faster than sales |
| Petty cash creep | Expenses paid from the till without receipts | Unexplained cash variances and "miscellaneous" costs |

None of these needs a sophisticated thief. Each needs a gap between the person who handles the money and the person who records it.

### Part 2: The Present-Absent Test

Before building any control, measure the problem.

$$
\text{Present-absent gap} = \frac{\text{Average daily sales on days you are present} - \text{Average daily sales on days you are absent}}{\text{Average daily sales on days you are absent}} \times 100
$$

Run it over at least eight weeks so that weekdays, paydays and market days balance out. Compare like with like: Saturdays with Saturdays, month-end with month-end.

**Worked example.** An agrovet averages KES 38,000 a day on the eight Saturdays the owner was in the shop and KES 31,500 on the eight Saturdays they were not. The gap is 20.6%. Across a month of roughly KES 900,000 in absent-day sales, a gap of that size suggests about KES 185,000 of sales that are either not happening or not being recorded.

A gap is not proof of dishonesty. Owners sell more because they know the stock, push the higher-margin lines and close more conversations. But a persistent gap of more than a few percent deserves explanation, and a gap that widens over time almost always has one.

### Part 3: Separate the Roles

The oldest control in accounting is also the most useful in a small shop: **the person who takes the money should not be the only person who records it, and neither of them should be the person who counts it at the end of the day.** In a business of two or three staff you cannot separate every role, but you can separate enough.

- **The seller records.** Every sale goes into the till system, the receipt book, or the point-of-sale app at the moment it happens.
- **The cashier holds.** Where you have two people, one takes the cash and the other makes the sale.
- **Someone else counts.** At close, a different person counts cash, or the count is done on a video call with you.
- **You reconcile.** Remotely, every day, from records the counter staff cannot edit after the fact.

Where a business has only one attendant, the owner's daily remote reconciliation becomes the second pair of hands. That is why it has to happen every day, not when you have time.

### Part 4: The Daily Close

The daily close is the routine that turns a day's trading into numbers you can trust. It should take fifteen minutes and happen at the same time every day.

1. **Print or screenshot the system sales report** for the day, by payment method: cash, M-Pesa till or paybill, card.
2. **Count the cash blind.** The person counting writes down the cash in the drawer without seeing the system's expected figure.
3. **Deduct the opening float.** A fixed float, the same every day, makes this trivial.
4. **Compare counted cash with expected cash.** The difference is the cash variance.
5. **Check digital sales against the provider statement,** not against what staff say they received. The M-Pesa statement or card settlement report is the truth.
6. **Record voids, refunds and discounts** with the reason and the approver.
7. **Bank the cash** the same day or the next morning, into the business account, keeping the deposit slip.
8. **Send it all to the owner** in a fixed format.

$$
\text{Cash variance} = \text{Counted cash} - \text{Opening float} - \text{Expected cash sales} + \text{Recorded cash expenses}
$$

**Set a tolerance and enforce it.** A small variance is normal. Coins short, change errors, rounding. Set a limit, for example KES 200 a day or 0.5% of cash sales, whichever is larger. Within the limit, record it. Outside it, the person responsible explains it the same day, in writing. A variance that is consistently just inside the limit, or that always runs in the same direction, is a signal in itself.

**The blind count matters more than it looks.** If the person counting knows the expected figure, they will find it. If they do not, the count tells you what is actually in the drawer.

### Part 5: Make Cash the Exception

The easiest cash to control is cash that never enters the drawer.

**Route digital payments to an account you control.** A business till or paybill that settles to the business bank account, displayed clearly at the counter, means a digital sale cannot land in an attendant's personal wallet. Make it a rule that staff never accept payment to any other number, and tell customers so on a sign at the till. The [M-Pesa for business piece](/blog/mpesa-for-business-paybill-till-kenya) covers which product to use and how settlement works.

**Bank cash daily.** Cash that sits in the shop overnight is a security risk and a temptation. A daily deposit, with the slip photographed and sent to you, converts cash into a bank record you can see.

**Never pay expenses from the till.** Run an **imprest petty cash system** instead: a fixed petty cash float, for example KES 5,000, kept separately from the till. Expenses are paid from it against receipts, and when it runs low it is topped up by exactly the amount of the receipts presented. At any moment, cash plus receipts should equal the float.

### Part 6: Stock Is Cash on a Shelf

In a retail business, most of your money sits on shelves, not in the drawer. A shop that controls its cash perfectly and its stock loosely has simply moved the leak.

$$
\text{Shrinkage rate} = \frac{\text{Book stock value} - \text{Physical stock value}}{\text{Sales for the period}} \times 100
$$

**Count the fast movers often.** You do not need a full stock-take every week. Pick the 20 to 30 lines that sell fastest or are easiest to pocket, and count them weekly, on a day staff do not know in advance. Do a full count monthly or quarterly.

**Receive deliveries properly.** Count goods against the delivery note before signing. A signature on a short delivery turns a supplier's error, or a supplier's collusion, into your loss. Our [data-driven inventory piece](/blog/data-driven-inventory-small-shops-kenya) covers reorder points and slow-moving stock; here the point is only that stock records are a cash control.

**Watch gross margin.** If sales are steady and purchase prices have not changed, gross margin should be steady too. A margin that drifts down is often the first visible sign of unrecorded sales, unauthorised discounts or stock leaving the building.

### Part 7: Technology That Pays for Itself

You do not need an enterprise system. You need records the counter staff cannot quietly change, and a way to see them from wherever you are.

- **A point-of-sale app** on a phone or tablet that logs every sale, void and discount with a time and a user, and sends a daily report to your phone.
- **eTIMS-compliant invoicing,** which you need anyway for tax and which creates a sales record outside the shop. See our [eTIMS guide](/blog/etims-kenya-sme-guide).
- **CCTV covering the till and the stockroom,** viewable remotely. Tell staff and customers it is there with clear signage, and handle the footage as personal data. The [data protection piece](/blog/data-protection-smes-odpc-kenya) explains what that requires.
- **Bank and M-Pesa statement access** on your own phone, not through a staff member.

The test for any tool is simple: does it give you a record you can check without asking the person being checked?

### Part 8: The Human Side

Controls work best in a business where staff have a reason to want them to work.

- **Pay fairly and on time.** A shop attendant earning less than they need to live on is under pressure every day they handle your cash.
- **Make the routine routine.** The daily close, the blind count and the deposit slip should be how the business runs, not a reaction to suspicion.
- **Rotate and require leave.** Many leaks need the same person in the same seat every day to keep them hidden. A colleague covering for a week tends to expose them.
- **Make surprise visits genuinely unannounced.** Arrive at different times and days, count the till, and check a few stock lines.
- **Reward clean results.** A small bonus for a month within variance limits and below a shrinkage target aligns incentives cheaply.
- **Act on shortages lawfully.** The Employment Act restricts what an employer may deduct from wages. Do not dock shortages from salaries without checking what the Act allows, and put any agreed arrangement in writing. Where you suspect theft, document the evidence and take advice before acting.

> **Risk Factors:** The highest-severity risk is a long-running leak that the owner discovers only when the business needs a loan or cannot pay a supplier, by which time months of records are unreliable. The most likely risk is control fatigue: the owner checks the daily close carefully for the first month and then stops, and staff notice. Digital payments routed to a personal number are a fast-growing leak because they leave the cash count untouched. CCTV and staff monitoring create data protection obligations if handled carelessly. And acting on a suspicion without evidence, or deducting shortages unlawfully, can turn a cash problem into an employment dispute.

### Bengula View

From the banking side, the absentee-owned business is one of the hardest credit cases to assess. The owner is often an excellent borrower personally, with a salary and a clean record. The business is opaque: sales records that do not match the bank statements, cash that never reaches the account, and stock no one has counted in a year. When that owner asks for working capital, the honest answer is often that the business does not need more money. It needs to stop losing what it already makes.

We would weigh two things most heavily. First, get every shilling you can into the bank, through a business till or paybill and a daily cash deposit. That one habit fixes visibility, builds the transaction history a lender wants to see, and removes most of the opportunity for leakage at once. Second, make the daily close non-negotiable and look at it every day. Controls that the owner stops checking stop working within weeks.

Done well, these controls are not a burden on good staff. They protect them. When there is a shortage, a clean daily record shows who was on shift and what happened, and the honest attendant is not left under a cloud of general suspicion.

### Conclusion

You cannot stand in every shop you own, but you can see into it. Measure the gap between your present and absent days, separate the roles that matter, close every day with a blind count and a deposit, route digital payments to the business, and count the stock that walks. None of it is expensive. All of it depends on the owner looking, every day. The business that pays its owner is usually not the one with the best location. It is the one where the numbers at the counter are the numbers in the bank.

### Frequently Asked Questions

**How much cash variance is normal?** A small daily variance is normal. Set a tolerance, for example KES 200 or 0.5% of cash sales, whichever is larger, and require a same-day written explanation for anything beyond it. Watch the pattern as well as the size.

**My only attendant is a relative. Do I still need controls?** Yes, arguably more so, because family relationships make problems harder to raise later. Present the daily close as how the business runs, not as a judgement on anyone.

**Can I deduct shortages from salary?** Only within what the Employment Act allows. Check the Act and put any arrangement in writing before you make a deduction, and take advice where theft is suspected.

**Is CCTV enough?** No. Cameras show what happened at the counter. They do not tell you a sale was not recorded or that stock is missing. Use them alongside the daily close and stock counts, and handle the footage under the Data Protection Act.

**What is the single most useful control?** Routing every payment you can into the business bank account, through a business till or paybill and a daily cash deposit. It shrinks the cash you have to control and creates a record no one at the counter can edit.
