Here is a number: **3.53%**. That is what the average Kenyan savings account paid as at July 2026, and to most savers it reads as a small but positive return. Money went in, slightly more money came out, nothing was lost.

Here is the same number told properly. After withholding tax it becomes 3.0%. Against inflation running at **6.6%**, it becomes **minus 3.38%**. Not a small gain. A guaranteed annual loss of purchasing power of more than three per cent, compounding, on money the saver believes is being kept safe.

That gap between the advertised rate and what you actually keep is the subject of this guide, and it is the signature move of this pillar: **take a headline yield, subtract everything that is really taken from it, and land on the number that matters.** It applies to every instrument you will ever be offered. Once you can do it in your head, a great deal of financial marketing stops working on you.

> **Key Insight:** There are two deductions between the quoted rate and your real return, and savers routinely account for neither. **Tax** takes a fixed share of the nominal yield. **Inflation** takes a share of everything, including the part the tax already took. An instrument only builds wealth if what survives both is positive, and in Kenya as at September 2026 several of the most popular places to keep money do not clear that bar.

### Part 1: Nominal, Net, and Real

Three different numbers, and most conversations confuse at least two of them.

**Nominal** is the advertised rate. It is the number on the poster, the fund fact sheet, the auction result.

**Net** is what remains after tax. In Kenya most interest income is subject to withholding tax, commonly **15%** on bank deposits, SACCO interest, money market funds and Treasury bills. Some instruments are treated differently, and infrastructure bonds are exempt where the tenor qualifies. The full cross-instrument table, including the 10% rate on longer Treasury bonds, sits in [the fixed income guide](/blog/fixed-income-kenya-complete-guide).

**Real** is what remains after inflation, and it is the only one that tells you whether you got richer.

The relationship is the Fisher equation, and it is worth doing properly rather than by subtraction:

$$
r_{\text{real}} = \frac{1 + r_{\text{net}}}{1 + \pi} - 1
$$

where $r_{\text{net}}$ is the after-tax return and $\pi$ is inflation. Simple subtraction is a reasonable approximation at low rates and drifts as rates rise, so use the formula when the numbers matter.

Worked on the savings account, with inflation at **6.6%** (August 2026):

$$
r_{\text{net}} = 3.53\% \times (1 - 0.15) = 3.00\%
$$

$$
r_{\text{real}} = \frac{1.0300}{1.0660} - 1 = -3.38\%
$$

**Minus 3.38%.** That is the true annual return on the most widely held savings product in the country, and nobody advertises it.

### Part 2: The Full Stack, Applied Across the Options

Now run the same three steps across the instruments a Kenyan saver actually chooses between. All figures are as at the dates given, with inflation at 6.6% (August 2026).

| Instrument | Nominal | Withholding | Net of tax | **Real return** |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Average savings account (Jul 2026) | 3.53% | 15% | 3.00% | **-3.38%** |
| Average bank deposit (Jul 2026) | 6.93% | 15% | 5.89% | **-0.67%** |
| 91-day Treasury bill (21 Sep 2026) | 8.784% | 15% | 7.47% | **+0.81%** |
| 364-day Treasury bill (10 Sep 2026) | 9.067% | 15% | 7.71% | **+1.04%** |
| Long Treasury bond, indicative | 13.167% | 15% | 11.19% | **+4.31%** |
| Infrastructure bond, indicative | 12.737% | **exempt** | 12.74% | **+5.76%** |

Read that table slowly, because several things in it are counterintuitive.

**Two of the six options destroy purchasing power.** A savings account loses 3.38% a year in real terms. A fixed deposit, the instrument people move to when they want to be more serious, still loses 0.67%. Both are described by the institutions selling them as ways to grow your money.

**The step from a bank to a bill is enormous.** Moving from the average savings rate to a 91-day Treasury bill is a swing of more than four percentage points of real return, from -3.38% to +0.81%, for the same short-term commitment to a better counterparty. This is the arithmetic behind the asymmetry described in [the CBK rate decision guide](/blog/how-to-read-cbk-rate-decision-kenya), and it is available to any individual through DhowCSD, as [the T-bill ladder guide](/blog/advanced-dhowcsd-t-bill-ladder) sets out.

**The lower headline rate wins.** Look at the last two rows. A Treasury bond at **13.167%** produces a smaller real return than an infrastructure bond at **12.737%**, because the first is taxed and the second is not. This is the tax-equivalent yield point, and it is the single most valuable piece of arithmetic in Kenyan fixed income. Anyone comparing instruments on headline yield alone will reliably choose the worse one.

**Even the good outcomes are modest.** The best figure in that table is 5.76%. Real returns in the low-to-mid single digits are what a conservative Kenyan portfolio produces, and expectations should be set accordingly. Anything promising far more is either taking risk it has not disclosed or is not telling the truth.

### Part 3: Why the Savings Account Is the Expensive Choice

It deserves its own section because so much money sits there.

A savings account paying 3.53% against inflation of 6.6% is not a cautious choice. It is a **guaranteed** real loss, and the guarantee is the unusual part: with most risks you might be lucky. Here the outcome is arithmetic. Leave KES 1,000,000 in such an account for five years and, in purchasing power, you will have roughly **KES 842,000** of today's money. You will see a balance that has grown, and you will be able to buy less with it.

The three reasons people still do it are worth naming honestly, because two of them are legitimate.

**Liquidity.** You want instant access. This is a real requirement, and an emergency fund genuinely belongs somewhere immediate. But an emergency fund is a few months of expenses, not a life's savings, and even that can usually sit in a money market fund with next-day access instead. [The bank, SACCO and MMF comparison](/blog/bank-vs-sacco-vs-mmf-savings) covers the trade properly.

**Not knowing the alternative exists.** Many savers have never been shown that a 91-day Treasury bill is available to individuals in modest amounts. That is an information gap, and it is fixable in an afternoon.

**Inertia.** The account was opened years ago, the money accumulated, and nobody sat down to do this arithmetic. This is the big one, and it is the reason this article exists.

The instruction is narrow and it is not "take more risk". It is: **keep the emergency fund liquid, and stop leaving everything else in an instrument that loses 3.4% a year by design.** That is not an investing decision. It is an admin decision.

### Part 4: The Shilling, and Why It Is Your Problem Too

Currency is usually presented as an exporter's concern. For a Kenyan household it is a cost-of-living issue, and it belongs in any honest account of real returns.

The shilling stood at about **KES 129.46 to the US dollar as at 28 August 2026**. That single number reaches ordinary households through several channels, none of which involve a trading screen.

**Imported costs.** Fuel, cooking oil, wheat, pharmaceuticals, vehicles, electronics and most industrial inputs are priced in dollars. When the shilling weakens, those prices rise in shillings regardless of what happens abroad, and that feeds straight into the inflation figure that sits in the denominator of every calculation above. A weaker shilling is, in part, how inflation is manufactured.

**Obligations abroad.** School fees overseas, foreign medical treatment, dollar-denominated debt and international subscriptions are all liabilities in a currency you do not earn. If you have a genuine dollar liability coming, you have a genuine dollar exposure now, and that is a planning question rather than a market view.

**Offshore holdings.** If you hold foreign assets, your return has two parts: what the asset did, and what the currency did. A modest gain abroad can become a good one in shillings if the shilling weakens, and a solid gain abroad can be erased if it strengthens. [The offshore investing guide](/blog/foreign-etfs-offshore-investing-kenya) covers the routes; the point here is that the currency component is not a detail, it is frequently the larger of the two.

**The household framing.** Currency exposure is not a trade you put on. It is a question about whether the money you will need is in the same currency as the money you have. A household with a dollar obligation in two years and everything in shillings has taken a position, whether or not it intended to. So has a household with dollar savings and only shilling spending. The discipline is to **match the currency of your assets to the currency of your future obligations**, and to treat any deliberate mismatch as a decision you have made rather than a situation you are in.

What this is not is a licence to speculate. The SME treasury version of this problem, with actual hedging instruments and a corporate FX policy, is covered in [the USD/KES hedging guide](/blog/usd-shilling-hedging). For a household, matching is the whole of the technique.

> **Risk Factors:** The errors that cost the most here are comparing a tax-exempt yield with a taxable one on headline numbers; assuming inflation affects only the things you notice getting more expensive; treating a fixed deposit as safe because the nominal number cannot fall, while the real number is negative; taking currency risk by accident rather than by decision; and reaching for a much higher advertised return without asking what risk is producing it, which is how people end up in the schemes [the investment evaluation guide](/blog/evaluate-investment-opportunity-kenya) was written to screen out.

### Decision Framework: Running the Stack on Any Offer

**What is the nominal rate, stated annually?** Insist on an annual figure. A monthly or per-period rate is how unattractive returns are disguised.

**What tax applies?** Usually 15% withholding on interest, but not always. Exempt instruments are worth materially more than their headline suggests.

**What is current inflation?** Use the latest published figure, 6.6% as at August 2026, and update it. This number belongs in every calculation you do.

**Run the formula.** Net, then real. If the real number is negative, the product is a cost, not an investment, and should be justified by something other than return, such as liquidity.

**What is the currency of the money and of my future needs?** If they differ, that is a position. Decide whether you meant to take it.

**What am I comparing it against?** The honest benchmark for any low-risk Kenyan product is a Treasury bill, because it is available, liquid, and its rate is published weekly.

### Bengula View

Three observations from the desk.

First, **the savings account is the most expensive safe place in Kenya, and almost nobody has done the arithmetic.** Minus 3.38% a year, guaranteed, on the product most households use as their default. The tragedy is that the fix requires no additional risk at all: the same money in a 91-day Treasury bill earns a positive real return with a better counterparty and near-equivalent access. This is not a sophisticated observation. It is an arithmetic one, and the reason it persists is that nobody is paid to tell you.

Second, **the tax-exempt comparison is where the most money is left on the table by careful people.** A bond at 13.167% paying less, in real terms, than one at 12.737% is exactly the kind of result that defeats intuition, and it catches sensible investors who compare rates diligently and simply compare the wrong number. If you take one habit from this piece, take this one: **never compare two yields until you have put them on the same tax footing.**

Third, **inflation is the deduction nobody itemises.** Tax at least appears on a statement, so people resent it and account for it. Inflation never appears anywhere. It takes its share silently, every year, from every shilling, and it is currently taking 6.6%, which is more than most of the instruments in the table above are paying after tax. A household that tracks fees to the last shilling while ignoring a 6.6% annual deduction has its attention in exactly the wrong place.

### Conclusion

Every rate you are quoted is gross. Between it and you sit two deductions: a withholding tax that takes a fixed share, and inflation that takes a share of everything left. Run both, in that order, and you get the only number that answers the question of whether you are actually better off.

Run as at September 2026, with inflation at 6.6%, the results are stark. A savings account at 3.53% returns **minus 3.38%** in real terms. A fixed deposit at 6.93% returns **minus 0.67%**. A 91-day Treasury bill at 8.784% returns **plus 0.81%**. And a tax-exempt infrastructure bond at 12.737% returns **plus 5.76%**, beating a Treasury bond with a higher headline rate.

So do three things. Run the stack on anything you are offered before you accept it. Move whatever is not genuinely an emergency fund out of instruments with negative real returns, which requires no additional risk, only an afternoon. And look at the currency of your future obligations rather than only at the currency of your present balance, because a mismatch you never decided on is still a position you hold.

### Frequently Asked Questions

**What is a real return?** What is left after inflation. Use the Fisher relationship, net return plus one, divided by inflation plus one, minus one, rather than simple subtraction when the numbers matter.

**Is withholding tax always 15%?** It is the common rate on bank interest, SACCO interest, money market funds and Treasury bills, but it is not universal. Longer Treasury bonds are treated differently, and qualifying infrastructure bonds are exempt. Check the instrument, and confirm current treatment with KRA.

**Is my savings account really losing money?** In purchasing power, yes, on these figures. At 3.53% nominal, 3.00% after tax, against 6.6% inflation, the real return is about minus 3.38% a year.

**So should I put everything in Treasury bills?** No. Keep genuine emergency money immediately accessible, and match what you hold to when you will need it. The point is that money which is not an emergency fund should not sit in an instrument with a negative real return by default.

**Does a weaker shilling make me poorer?** It raises the shilling price of imported goods and any obligation you have in foreign currency, and it raises the shilling value of foreign assets you hold. Whether you are better or worse off depends on which side of that you sit, which is why matching assets to future obligations is the whole technique.

**How often should I redo this calculation?** Whenever inflation is republished, monthly, or whenever you are deciding where to place money. The inputs are all public.
