Every week the Central Bank runs a Treasury bill auction and publishes three rates: the 91-day, the 182-day and the 364-day. Almost everybody who looks at them does the same thing, which is to find the biggest number and buy that one.

That instinct is not wrong exactly, but it throws away the information. Those three numbers are not three separate products competing on price. They are three points on a curve, and **the shape of the curve is a statement about what the market expects to happen next**, and about whether you are being paid anything worthwhile for tying your money up longer.

This guide reads that shape. It covers what a normal, flat and inverted curve each mean, how to use the shape to choose a ladder tenor rather than guessing, and how to read an auction result properly, because the headline rate is the least interesting line in the results table. The instrument mechanics, coupons, duration, tax treatment, sit in [the fixed income guide](/blog/fixed-income-kenya-complete-guide); this is about reading the market those instruments trade in.

> **Key Insight:** The curve pays you for two things, expected future rates and the risk of committing money for longer. When the gap between the short end and the long end is small, the market is telling you it does not expect much to change, and that it is not willing to pay you for a lock-up. At that point the *correct* response is usually to stay short, keep the optionality, and stop reaching for a few basis points that cost you a year of flexibility.

### Part 1: What the Curve Is

A yield curve plots the return on otherwise identical instruments against how long you commit your money. For Kenyan Treasury bills, that is three points, 91 days, 182 days and 364 days. Extend it into Treasury bonds and you get the rest of the curve out to twenty-five years or more.

The crucial property is that **the instruments are the same in every respect except time.** Same issuer, the Government of Kenya. Same credit risk. Same mechanism. So any difference in yield between them is a pure price on **duration**, and that makes the differences readable in a way that comparisons across different products never are.

What that price consists of is two things bundled together:

**Expectations.** If the market believes rates will be higher in a year, it will demand more to lock in for a year, because the alternative is to stay short and reinvest at the better rate later. So a rising curve encodes an expectation of rising rates, and a falling one encodes the opposite.

**A term premium.** Separately from expectations, investors generally want compensation for giving up flexibility and for the risk of being wrong. This is why the normal state of a curve is mildly upward-sloping even when nobody expects much to change.

Those two components cannot be cleanly separated from the outside, and anyone who tells you exactly how much of a spread is expectation and how much is premium is guessing. What you can read reliably is the **shape**, and what the shape implies for your own decision.

### Part 2: Reading the Three Shapes

**A normal curve slopes upward.** Longer money pays more. This is the ordinary state of the world and it means the system is working as designed: you are compensated for commitment. When the slope is meaningful, laddering out into longer tenors is genuinely paid for, and the extra yield is a reason rather than a rationalisation.

**A flat curve is the interesting one, and it is the one Kenya has right now.** Long rates sit barely above short rates. The market is saying two things at once: it does not expect much movement, and it is not prepared to pay much for your commitment. The practical implication is the opposite of most people's instinct. When the curve is flat, **reaching for the long tenor is a bad trade**, because you are giving up a great deal of flexibility for a trivial gain. Staying short and reinvesting costs you almost nothing and keeps every option open.

**An inverted curve, where short rates exceed long, is rare and loud.** It means the market expects rates to be materially lower in future, which usually means it expects conditions to weaken enough to force cuts. In an inversion, the calculus flips: locking in the long rate before it disappears becomes the rational move, even though the long rate looks lower today, because the short rate you are comparing it against is the one that will not survive.

The one discipline that applies in all three states: **the curve is a description, not a forecast.** It tells you what the market currently believes, and the market is frequently wrong. Its value is not that it predicts, but that it prices, so you can see exactly what you are being offered and decide whether it is enough.

### Part 3: The Curve Right Now, and What It Is Saying

Take the Treasury bill auction of **10 September 2026**.

| Tenor | Accepted rate | Extra days vs 91-day | Extra yield vs 91-day |
|---|---|---|---|
| 91-day | **8.767%** | baseline | baseline |
| 182-day | **8.930%** | 91 days | **16.3 basis points** |
| 364-day | **9.067%** | 273 days | **30.0 basis points** |

That is the whole curve in three lines, and it is remarkably flat. **Committing your money for an extra 273 days buys you thirty basis points**, which is 0.30 of a percentage point.

Make it concrete. On **KES 1,000,000**, the 364-day bill pays about **KES 3,000 more over the year** than rolling the 91-day three times at the same rate would. Three thousand shillings, for giving up access to a million for nine additional months. And that comparison flatters the long bill, because it assumes the 91-day rate does not rise at all over the year; if it does, the roller wins outright.

For context on how tight this is, the policy rate is **8.75%** and the 91-day bill is sitting at **8.767%**, essentially on top of it. The entire Kenyan bill curve is contained within about thirty basis points above the Central Bank Rate. That is a market with a settled view and very little to say.

So the honest reading for a saver is: **there is currently almost no reward for extending along the bill curve.** Stay short, keep your optionality, and revisit when the shape changes. That is a genuinely useful conclusion, and it is invisible to anyone who only looked at the 9.067% and concluded that the 364-day was the best bill.

One important boundary. This flatness applies to the **bill curve**, out to one year. The bond curve is a different matter: longer government bonds, and especially tax-exempt infrastructure bonds, have been paying materially more than anything on this table, and the tax treatment changes the comparison again. That arithmetic is worked through in [the fixed income guide](/blog/fixed-income-kenya-complete-guide). What is flat here is the short end, and what follows is about the short end.

### Part 4: How to Read an Auction Result

The rate is the last thing to look at, not the first. A published auction result contains four numbers per tenor, and the other three tell you more.

Using the **10 September 2026** auction:

| | 91-day | 182-day | 364-day | Total |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Advertised | KES 8.0bn | KES 10.0bn | KES 10.0bn | **KES 28.0bn** |
| Bids received | KES 29.58bn | KES 12.97bn | KES 12.96bn | **KES 55.5bn** |
| Bid-to-cover | **3.70x** | 1.30x | 1.30x | **1.98x** |
| Accepted | KES 29.39bn | KES 11.31bn | KES 12.58bn | |
| Rate | 8.767% | 8.930% | 9.067% | |

**Bid-to-cover is demand.** It is bids received divided by the amount advertised. A ratio comfortably above 1 means the auction was covered; a high ratio means the government could have raised far more than it asked for. Here the overall auction drew **KES 55.5 billion against KES 28.0 billion advertised**, a performance of about 198%. That is a liquid market with money looking for a home.

**The distribution of that demand is the real story.** The 91-day drew a bid-to-cover of **3.70 times**, while both longer tenors drew 1.30. Investors are crowding into the short end, which is exactly the behaviour you would expect given the curve in Part 3: when the long end pays you almost nothing extra, nobody wants it. **The shape of the curve and the shape of the demand are the same fact stated twice.**

**Accepted versus received shows the government's hand.** At the 91-day, the Treasury accepted KES 29.39 billion against an advertised KES 8 billion, taking nearly four times what it asked for because the money was cheap and abundant. At the 182-day it accepted KES 11.31 billion out of KES 12.97 billion bid, rejecting the rest. **Rejected bids are bids at rates the government would not pay**, and a pattern of rejections at a tenor tells you the Treasury is defending a rate ceiling there.

**Only then look at the rate**, and look at it as a change rather than a level. The 91-day came in one basis point below the previous auction. On its own that is noise; as part of a run of weekly prints it is a trend.

The practical habit: **check bid-to-cover, check acceptance, then check the rate, then check the shape.** Four numbers, two minutes a week, and you will understand the short end of the Kenyan market better than most people who talk about it.

### Part 5: Choosing Your Ladder Tenor From the Shape

This is where the curve stops being interesting and starts being useful. A ladder, holding bills across staggered maturities so something matures regularly, is the standard structure, and [the T-bill ladder guide](/blog/advanced-dhowcsd-t-bill-ladder) covers the construction. The question the curve answers is **how far out to build it**.

**When the curve is flat, as now: stay short and build a tight ladder.** If 273 extra days pay thirty basis points, the extra yield does not justify the lock-up. A 91-day ladder gives you four reinvestment points a year, complete flexibility, and gives up almost nothing. This is the rare situation where the cautious choice and the optimal choice are the same.

**When the curve steepens meaningfully: extend.** A genuine premium for duration is a reason to ladder further out and capture it. The steeper it is, the more the extra tenor is actually being paid for.

**When the curve inverts: lock in the long end while you can.** This feels backwards, because you are accepting a lower nominal rate. But the short rate you are giving up is precisely the one the market expects to fall, and the choice is between a lower rate you keep for a year and a higher rate that disappears at the next reset.

**In every state, the question to ask is the same:** what am I being paid for the commitment, and is it enough to give up the option? Write the number down in shillings rather than basis points, on the amount you actually hold. Thirty basis points sounds like something. Three thousand shillings on a million, for nine months of illiquidity, sounds like what it is.

> **Risk Factors:** The recurring errors are reaching for the longest tenor purely because the number is highest, without pricing the flexibility you are selling. Reading one week's auction as a trend. Comparing a taxable yield with a tax-exempt one without adjusting, which flatters the taxable instrument and is dealt with in the fixed income guide. Building a ladder longer than your actual liquidity needs, so that you are forced to sell into the secondary market at a discount. And assuming the flat bill curve means the whole Kenyan yield curve is flat; the bond end is a different conversation.

### Decision Framework: Before Your Next Bill Purchase

**What is the actual spread, in shillings, on my amount?** Convert basis points into money before deciding anything. It reframes the decision instantly.

**What shape is the curve in?** Flat argues for short. Steep argues for extending. Inverted argues for locking in.

**When do I actually need this money?** The curve can only tell you what extension is worth; it cannot tell you whether you can afford it. Liquidity need comes first.

**What did the bid-to-cover look like?** Heavy demand at the short end and thin demand further out is the market agreeing with the flat-curve reading.

**Am I comparing like with like?** A tax-exempt infrastructure bond and a taxable bill are not comparable on headline yield. Adjust before concluding.

### Bengula View

Three observations from the desk.

First, **the flat curve is the most actionable thing in the Kenyan market right now, and it is being almost entirely ignored.** Thirty basis points between the 91-day and the 364-day means the market is offering you nothing for a year of commitment. The right response is not sophisticated: stay short, stay liquid, roll. Savers keep reaching for the 364-day because it prints the highest number, and they are paying nine months of flexibility for three thousand shillings on a million. When the curve steepens, extend. Until then, do not.

Second, **the bid-to-cover data is public, free, weekly, and nobody reads it.** A 3.70 times cover at the 91-day against 1.30 at both longer tenors is the market voting with real money on exactly the question this article asks, and it takes ten seconds to find. If you want one habit from this piece, it is to look at demand by tenor before you look at rates by tenor. Demand tells you where the informed money is going; rates only tell you what it cost.

Third, **be careful about generalising from the short end.** A flat bill curve does not mean there is nothing worth owning in Kenyan fixed income. It means the sub-one-year market is settled and unexciting, which is genuinely useful to know, and which sits alongside a long end where tax-exempt infrastructure paper has been paying substantially more. Two different markets, two different conversations, and conflating them produces bad decisions in both directions.

### Conclusion

Three rates get published every week and they make a shape. The shape tells you what the market expects and, more usefully, what it is willing to pay you for tying your money up.

As at the auction of 10 September 2026, the Kenyan bill curve is flat: 8.767% at 91 days, 8.930% at 182, 9.067% at 364. Thirty basis points for 273 extra days of commitment, on a policy rate of 8.75%. Demand confirms the reading, with the short end drawing nearly three times the cover of either longer tenor. The market is settled and it is not paying for patience.

So read the shape before the number, convert the spread into shillings before deciding, check bid-to-cover before checking rates, and build the ladder the curve is actually paying for rather than the one with the biggest headline. When the shape changes, and it will, change with it.

### Frequently Asked Questions

**Should I just always buy the 364-day because it pays most?** Not when the curve is this flat. Thirty basis points over the 91-day means you are giving up nine months of access for very little. Decide by the spread in shillings on your own amount, not by which number is highest.

**What is bid-to-cover?** Bids received divided by the amount advertised. It measures demand. At the 10 September 2026 auction the 91-day drew 3.70 times cover while both longer tenors drew 1.30.

**Why did the government accept more than it advertised?** Because the money was abundant and cheap. Accepting KES 29.39 billion at the 91-day against KES 8 billion advertised is a choice to take advantage of demand. Conversely, rejecting bids at a tenor signals a rate the Treasury is not willing to pay.

**Does a flat bill curve mean bonds are unattractive too?** No. The bill curve covers maturities up to one year. The bond end, especially tax-exempt infrastructure bonds, is priced separately and has been paying materially more. See the fixed income guide.

**How often should I check the curve?** Auctions are weekly, but decisions do not need to be. Look at it when you have money to place or a maturity coming up, and treat single-week moves as noise.
