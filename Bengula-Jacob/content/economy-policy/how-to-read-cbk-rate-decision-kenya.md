Every couple of months the same thing happens. The Central Bank of Kenya's Monetary Policy Committee meets, announces a number, and the headlines write themselves: rates held, rates cut, rates raised. Then everyone moves on, and the number sits there doing real work on real money, including yours, mostly unobserved.

The gap is not that people cannot follow the news. It is that almost nobody is taught the **mechanics**: what the committee is actually deciding, how that decision physically reaches a loan in Malindi or a money market fund in Nairobi, why those two things react on completely different timescales, and what a rational person should do at each end of the cycle.

This guide is that mechanism, written to stay true rather than to recap one meeting. It is deliberately evergreen: the worked examples are dated, but the plumbing described here does not change when the number does. For the positioning question, what to hold as the cycle turns, go to [the CBR cycle positioning guide](/blog/cbr-cycle-portfolio-positioning-kenya). For the detail of how your particular loan rate is built, go to [how Kenyan banks price loans](/blog/how-kenyan-banks-price-loans).

> **Key Insight:** The decision travels down two separate roads. The **credit channel** goes from the policy rate to the benchmark your loan is priced against, and reprices your debt. The **yield channel** goes from the policy rate to Treasury bill and bond yields, and reprices your savings, your money market fund and your bond portfolio. They move at different speeds, in different directions for different people, and confusing them is why so many households take exactly the wrong action after a rate announcement.

### Part 1: Who Decides, and What They Are Deciding

The Monetary Policy Committee is a defined body, not a mood.

**Composition.** The Governor chairs it. The Deputy Governor sits on it. Two further members are appointed by the Governor from within the Bank, one with executive responsibility for monetary policy analysis and one with responsibility for monetary policy operations. **Four external members** with expertise in finance, banking and fiscal and monetary policy are appointed from outside. The Principal Secretary to the National Treasury, or a representative, also sits on the committee but **does not vote**.

That last detail is worth pausing on. The non-voting Treasury seat is the institutional expression of central bank independence: the government is in the room and informed, and it does not get to set the rate.

**Frequency.** The MPC meets **at least once every two months**, reviewing data and analysis from the Bank's departments before deciding whether to maintain or vary its stance. In practice that is roughly six scheduled meetings a year, with dates published in advance.

**What they decide.** The headline output is the **Central Bank Rate**, the policy rate that anchors the price of money in the economy. It is not a rate you can borrow at, and it is not the rate on your loan. It is the signal and the anchor around which everything else is priced.

**What else they publish, and why it matters more than the number.** Alongside the decision comes a **press release** setting out the committee's reasoning: where inflation sits relative to the target band, what is happening to the shilling and to foreign exchange reserves, how credit to the private sector is growing, and how the committee reads the balance of risks. CBK also prepares a **Monetary Policy Statement** covering the next twelve months, specifying the policies and means by which it intends to hit its targets, its reasons, and an assessment of how the previous statement's implementation went.

**Read the reasoning, not the number.** The number tells you what happened. The reasoning tells you what is likely to happen next, which is the only part you can actually act on. A hold accompanied by language about inflation risks is a very different signal from a hold accompanied by language about weak private sector credit growth, and the two point to different next moves.

**Where things stand as at September 2026.** The CBR is **8.75%**, retained at the June 2026 meeting and again at the meeting of 11 August 2026. Inflation was **6.6% in August 2026**. The CBK discount window sits at **9.25%**, a quarter point above the policy rate, which is where banks go when they need overnight liquidity and which is why the corridor above the CBR exists.

### Part 2: The Credit Channel, From the CBR to Your Loan

This is the road most people have heard of, and it has an extra stage most people have not.

**The CBR anchors a benchmark, and the benchmark prices the loan.** Kenyan lending has moved to a risk-based model in which a loan rate is built as a **base plus a premium**: a market benchmark, plus a margin reflecting your specific credit risk. The benchmark in current use is **KESONIA**, an interbank-derived rate that tracks the actual cost of overnight money rather than a committee decision. The full construction, and what goes into the premium, is the subject of [the risk-based credit pricing guide](/blog/risk-based-credit-pricing-kesonia-cbr-kenya).

**Why the extra stage matters.** Because KESONIA is a market rate, it can move between MPC meetings, and it does not have to move exactly with the CBR. As at September 2026, KESONIA stood at **8.7497%** against a CBR of **8.75%**, which is to say almost exactly on top of it. When they sit this close, the distinction feels academic. It stops feeling academic whenever liquidity tightens and the market rate pulls away from the policy rate while the policy rate has not moved at all.

**The repricing is where it reaches your account.** A variable-rate loan reprices when the benchmark moves and the bank applies the change. That is the moment the MPC decision becomes a number on your statement. What happened to existing borrowers when the model changed, and what your bank must tell you, is covered in [the risk-based pricing guide](/blog/risk-based-credit-pricing-kesonia-cbr-kenya).

**Two important exceptions.** **Fixed-rate loans do not reprice at all.** Many scheme and government-backed facilities are fixed, which is a genuine feature rather than an oversight, and it means the cycle simply passes those borrowers by. And **mobile and digital loans largely ignore the policy rate**, because their pricing is driven by credit losses and acquisition costs rather than the cost of funds, which is why their effective rates stay startling through any cycle, as [the digital loans guide](/blog/mobile-digital-loans-real-cost-kenya) shows.

### Part 3: The Yield Channel, From the CBR to Your Savings

The second road is the one savers live on, and it runs through the government securities market rather than through banks' loan books.

**The policy rate anchors the short end of the curve.** Treasury bill yields, particularly the 91-day, sit close to the policy rate because they compete for the same short-term money. As at 21 September 2026 the **91-day Treasury bill was yielding 8.784%** against a CBR of 8.75%. When the MPC moves, the short end moves with it, usually quickly, because bills are reissued weekly at auction.

**Money market funds follow the bills.** An MMF holds short-dated paper and deposits, so its yield tracks what that paper pays, with a lag equal to how quickly the portfolio turns over. This is why MMF returns fall through a cutting cycle and rise through a tightening one, some months after the decision that caused it. [The MMF stress test](/blog/mmf-stress-test-kenya) covers how to read what a fund is actually holding, and [the future of MMFs piece](/blog/future-mmfs-kenya) covers where the product is heading.

**The long end is a different animal.** Longer bonds are priced on expectations of inflation and rates over many years, not on today's policy rate, which is why a cut at the short end does not reliably lower long yields. What the gap between short and long is telling you is the subject of the yield curve piece in this pillar, and the instrument-level detail sits in [the fixed income guide](/blog/fixed-income-kenya-complete-guide).

**And existing bondholders feel it in price, not yield.** If you hold a bond and rates rise, your coupon does not change but the market value of your holding falls, because new buyers can get a better rate elsewhere. That is duration, and on a long bond it is a large number.

### Part 4: The Asymmetry Nobody Announces

Here is the part that turns this from mechanics into something you can use.

**Rates do not pass through symmetrically.** When policy tightens, loan rates rise promptly, because the benchmark moves and variable loans reprice. When policy eases, loan rates fall, though usually with more friction. But **deposit rates are slower and stickier in both directions, and the savings rate is barely connected to policy at all.**

The Kenyan numbers make this unusually easy to see. As at July 2026:

| Rate | Level | Relationship to the 8.75% CBR |
|---|---|---|
| Average **lending** rate | **14.39%** | 5.64 percentage points **above** the policy rate |
| Average **deposit** rate | **6.93%** | 1.82 points **below** |
| Average **savings** rate | **3.53%** | 5.22 points **below** |
| 91-day **Treasury bill** | **8.784%** (21 Sep 2026) | Essentially **at** the policy rate |

Read the first and last rows together. **A bank lends at around 14.4% and pays an ordinary saver 3.53%.** Meanwhile the government, through a Treasury bill any individual can buy, pays 8.784% for the same short-term money. The gap between what your savings account pays and what a 91-day bill pays is not a market judgement about you; it is a fact about which counterparty you chose to lend to. How banks earn that spread, and why it exists, is the subject of [how banks actually make money](/blog/how-banks-actually-make-money-kenya).

The practical consequence is blunt. **If you are a borrower, the cycle reaches you quickly, so plan for it. If you are a saver in a bank account, the cycle barely reaches you at all, so stop waiting for it to.** The saver's gain from a tightening cycle has to be claimed deliberately, by moving money into instruments that actually track the policy rate, rather than received passively by leaving it where it is.

> **Risk Factors:** The errors here are predictable. Treating a single meeting as a trend, when the committee has held the same rate across consecutive meetings. Assuming a cut automatically lowers your loan, when your loan may be fixed, or priced off a market benchmark that has not moved. Assuming a rise automatically improves your savings, when the savings rate has barely tracked policy for years. Locking into a long fixed rate at the top of a cycle, or a long bond at the bottom of one. And reading a rate decision as a prediction of the economy rather than as a price change you must now respond to.

### Part 5: What to Do at Each End of the Cycle

The point of understanding the plumbing is to act on it. Two states, and the honest actions in each.

**When rates are high or rising:**

- **Borrowers:** avoid taking on new variable-rate debt you would struggle to service if it repriced again. Where a fixed rate is available on sensible terms, it has real value, though remember you are buying certainty rather than a cheap rate. Prioritise clearing the most rate-sensitive debt. If you have an overdraft that never clears, this is when it gets expensive, as [the overdraft guide](/blog/overdraft-that-never-clears-kenya) shows.
- **Savers:** this is when the yield channel pays you, and it pays through Treasury bills, money market funds and bonds, not through a savings account. Moving idle cash into instruments that track the policy rate is the single highest-value action available.
- **Bond buyers:** locking in a long tenor when yields are high is exactly the right instinct, and it is the one moment when duration works for you rather than against you.

**When rates are low or falling:**

- **Borrowers:** this is when refinancing and consolidation are worth running the numbers on, and [the debt consolidation guide](/blog/debt-consolidation-refinancing-kenya) sets out the break-even test. It is also when taking on long-term debt for a productive asset is least punishing.
- **Savers:** MMF and bill yields will drift down with a lag, and chasing the last few basis points between funds is usually not worth the effort. Reinvestment risk becomes the live problem: paper maturing into a lower-rate market.
- **Everyone:** this is the wrong end of the cycle to lock money away at long tenors, and the right end to have already done so.

**In either state, the same discipline applies.** Do not trade the announcement. The MPC meets at least every two months, decisions are published in advance, and a household portfolio restructured six times a year will lose more to friction and error than it gains from timing. [The CBR cycle positioning guide](/blog/cbr-cycle-portfolio-positioning-kenya) sets out a routine rather than a reflex, which is the right cadence.

### Decision Framework: After the Next Announcement

**Is my debt variable or fixed?** If fixed, most of this is noise for you until it matures. If variable, work out what a one-point move does to your monthly payment and keep that number where you can see it.

**Where is my idle cash sitting?** Compare what it earns against the 91-day bill. If the gap is several percentage points, that gap is a decision you are making by default.

**Did I read the statement or only the headline?** The reasoning tells you about the next meeting. The number only tells you about this one.

**Am I about to lock in a rate?** Ask which end of the cycle you are at, and who benefits from certainty on those terms, you or the counterparty.

**What would I do if the rate moved two points against me?** If the answer is "struggle", the exposure is too large regardless of what the committee does next.

### Bengula View

Three observations from the desk.

First, **the press release is worth more than the rate, and almost nobody reads it.** The decision is a single number that is usually anticipated. The committee's reasoning, on inflation relative to the band, on the shilling and reserves, on private sector credit growth, is a description of what would have to change for the next decision to be different. That is the only forward-looking information in the whole event, it is published free, and it takes ten minutes to read.

Second, **the asymmetry between lending and savings rates is the most under-discussed fact in Kenyan personal finance.** An average lending rate of 14.39% against an average savings rate of 3.53%, with a 91-day Treasury bill at 8.784% sitting between them, is not a subtle pattern. It says that the borrower is fully exposed to the policy cycle and the ordinary saver is almost entirely insulated from it, in the unhelpful direction. Savers who want the cycle to work for them have to move, deliberately, into the instruments where it actually operates. Nobody at your bank is going to suggest this.

Third, **treat rate decisions as a calendar, not as news.** The dates are published, the committee meets at least every two months, and the CBR has been held at 8.75% across consecutive meetings this year. That stability is itself the useful signal: it means the plumbing described above is doing routine work, and that your response should be a periodic review rather than a reaction. The households who do best through a cycle are the ones with a standing routine, not the ones with a strong opinion about the next meeting.

### Conclusion

A rate decision is not a comment on the economy. It is a price change, issued by a defined committee that meets at least every two months, that travels down two separate channels and lands on your money at two different speeds.

Down the credit channel, it reaches the benchmark your loan is priced against and reprices your debt, unless your rate is fixed, in which case it does not reach you at all. Down the yield channel, it reaches Treasury bill yields and therefore money market funds and bonds, and reprices your savings, but only if your savings are actually in those instruments rather than in a bank account paying 3.53%.

So the next time the announcement comes, do three things. Read the reasoning rather than the headline. Work out which channel you are exposed to and how fast. And then check whether your idle cash is earning something close to the policy rate or something closer to a third of it, because that gap is the largest single thing a rate decision will ever tell you about your own finances.

### Frequently Asked Questions

**How often does the MPC meet?** At least once every two months, with dates published in advance. The CBR was retained at 8.75% at both the June 2026 and 11 August 2026 meetings.

**Does a CBR cut mean my loan rate falls?** Only if your loan is variable and priced off a benchmark that moves with it. Fixed-rate loans, including many scheme loans, do not reprice. Digital and mobile loans are largely priced on credit losses rather than the cost of funds and barely respond at all.

**Why does my savings account not follow the CBR?** Because savings rates are set by banks competing for retail deposits, not by the policy rate. As at July 2026 the average savings rate was 3.53% while a 91-day Treasury bill paid 8.784%. The cycle reaches you through instruments, not through your current account.

**Who sits on the MPC?** The Governor as chair, the Deputy Governor, two internal members appointed by the Governor, four external members with relevant expertise, and the Principal Secretary to the Treasury or a representative, who does not vote.

**What is the difference between the CBR and KESONIA?** The CBR is the policy rate set by the committee. KESONIA is a market-derived rate reflecting the actual cost of overnight money, and it is the benchmark loans are priced against. They usually sit close together, and the difference matters most when liquidity conditions change between meetings.

**Should I move money right after a decision?** Rarely on the day. Build a periodic review instead. The instruments that track policy will still be there next week, and friction costs are certain in a way that timing gains are not.
