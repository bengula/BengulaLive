For most of the last decade, the honest answer to "is crypto legal in Kenya?" was uncomfortable: it was not illegal, it was not regulated, and if something went wrong you had very little recourse. People traded anyway, in large volumes, and the regulators issued warnings rather than rules.

That period is over. The **Virtual Asset Service Providers Act, 2025** commenced on **4 November 2025**, and with it Kenya acquired a licensing regime, two named regulators, and an obligation on platforms to report their users to the tax authority. The tax treatment changed too, and unusually for a tax change, it changed in favour of the taxpayer.

This guide is about what that framework actually says. It is deliberately not investment content: it takes no view on whether you should hold digital assets, contains no price commentary, and makes no predictions. It covers who regulates what, what you owe and when, why the genuinely interesting Kenyan use case is payments rather than speculation, and the risks that a licensing regime does not remove.

> **Key Insight:** Regulation changes who is accountable, not whether an asset can fall. The VASP Act tells you which regulator licenses a platform and what it must do; it does not make any digital asset safe, backed or suitable. The single most useful thing the framework gives an ordinary Kenyan is the ability to ask a specific question, "who licenses you, and under which Act?", and to treat the absence of a clear answer as the answer.

### Part 1: Who Regulates What

The Act does something sensible: it splits supervision by **function** rather than by asset, so the regulator you deal with depends on what the business actually does.

**The Capital Markets Authority** regulates the investment-shaped activities: virtual asset exchanges, virtual asset brokers, virtual asset investment advisers, virtual asset managers, and virtual asset offering providers. If it looks like trading, advising or raising money, it sits with the CMA.

**The Central Bank of Kenya** leads on the money-shaped activities: payments, custody and wallets. If it looks like holding value or moving it, it sits with the CBK.

That division matters practically, because it tells you **which regulator's register to check** for any provider you are considering, and which complaints route exists if something goes wrong.

**Licensing is still being switched on.** The Act commenced in November 2025, and licensing runs through implementing regulations, with the Virtual Asset Service Providers Regulations following in 2026 and the National Treasury developing the detailed requirements for licensing, supervision, risk controls and compliance. The consequence for anybody reading this in late 2026 is important and easily misunderstood: **a provider telling you it is "regulated under the VASP Act" is not the same as a provider telling you it holds a licence.** Ask which, and ask to see it.

**The transition window closes on 4 November 2026.** Providers already operating when the Act commenced were given one year to come into compliance. That deadline is weeks away as this is published, and it is the date after which "we are working on our application" stops being an acceptable answer.

**A word on MiCA**, since it comes up constantly. Europe's Markets in Crypto-Assets regulation is a useful reference point for how a mature framework handles stablecoin reserves, disclosure and authorisation, and Kenyan practice will doubtless borrow from it. But it is a comparison, not a standard that applies here, and a platform citing European compliance is telling you nothing about its position in Kenya. The law that governs you is the VASP Act and the regulations under it.

### Part 2: What You Actually Owe

The tax story is the part most people have wrong, because the rules changed materially and the old ones were widely publicised.

**What was repealed.** The **Digital Asset Tax**, introduced in 2023, charged **3% of the transaction value** of a transfer of a digital asset. It was a turnover tax, which meant it applied whether you made a gain or a loss, and on an actively traded position it compounded into a serious drag. Over roughly 21 months from September 2023 to June 2025 it raised about **KES 1.1 billion**.

**What replaced it.** From **1 July 2025**, the Finance Act 2025 repealed the DAT and introduced an **excise duty of 10% on the fees and commissions charged by virtual asset service providers**. The base changed completely: from the **value of what you transact** to the **fee the platform charges you**. Since platform fees are a small fraction of transaction value, this is a very large reduction in the tax burden on transacting, and it removes the perverse effect of taxing loss-making trades.

**What arrived in 2026.** The **Finance Act 2026**, assented on 23 June 2026 and gazetted on 26 June 2026, added reporting rather than a new charge. With effect from **1 July 2026**:

- Virtual asset service providers must **file annual information returns with KRA** on reportable users and controlling persons, drawing their definitions from the VASP Act 2025.
- Kenya may enter agreements with other jurisdictions for the **automatic exchange of information on virtual asset transactions**, including customer due diligence information and reportable accounts, which aligns Kenya with the international crypto-asset reporting direction of travel.
- Penalties attach: **KES 100,000** for a false statement, **KES 100,000** for an omission in an information return, and **KES 1,000,000** for failing to file.

Read those three together and the practical consequence is unambiguous. **Your platform activity is becoming visible to KRA, by law, including activity on platforms abroad once exchange agreements are in place.** Anyone who has treated digital assets as outside the tax system should assume that assumption has expired.

**And the tax that was never removed.** The repeal of the DAT was a repeal of one specific charge. It did not exempt digital assets from the rest of the tax system. Gains, trading profits and income remain subject to the ordinary rules, and how a particular position is characterised, trading income against a capital gain, is exactly the sort of question that depends on facts. [KRA's published position governs](https://www.kra.go.ke/), and where commentary conflicts with it, KRA wins. If you hold or trade at any scale, get your own position confirmed in writing rather than inferred from an article. The general compliance calendar sits in [the tax, compliance and cash guide](/blog/tax-compliance-cash-kenya-guide), and the capital gains framework in [capital gains tax in Kenya](/blog/capital-gains-tax-kenya).

### Part 3: The Use Case That Actually Matters Here

Most digital asset coverage in Kenya is about speculation. The more interesting Kenyan story is **payments**, and specifically stablecoins, because Kenya is a remittance economy with an expensive inbound corridor.

The problem is real and quantified elsewhere in this library. Money coming into Kenya from abroad loses value in two places, the fee and, larger and quieter, the exchange rate spread applied on the way in. [The guide to receiving money from abroad](/blog/receiving-money-from-abroad-kenya) works through the channels and makes the point that the fee is the small part and the rate is the cost.

A dollar-referenced stablecoin is, in principle, a way to move value across a border quickly and cheaply, converting to shillings at the destination. That is a genuine potential improvement on a corridor that has been expensive for decades, and it is why serious institutions, rather than only speculators, pay attention to stablecoins in African markets.

**Now the honest qualifications, because this is where enthusiasm outruns reality.**

**A stablecoin is a claim, not cash.** Its value depends entirely on the issuer actually holding the reserves it says it holds, and on being able to redeem at par when everyone asks at once. That is an issuer credit question, and it is the same category of question you would ask about any institution holding your money.

**You still have to get out at the other end.** The shilling conversion happens somewhere, through somebody, at some spread, and that spread is a cost like any other. A transfer is only cheap end to end, and the last mile is where cheapness frequently disappears.

**The regulated rails are improving too.** The comparison is not against the remittance costs of 2015. Mobile money, and the inbound channels compared in the receiving-money guide, have got faster and cheaper. Any honest comparison has to be against what is actually available to you today.

**And the regulatory position of payments sits with the CBK**, which is precisely the function the Act assigns to it. Payments, custody and wallets are the CBK's lane, so a provider offering stablecoin payment services into Kenya is operating in the most closely supervised part of this framework.

The reasonable conclusion is neither dismissal nor enthusiasm. **Stablecoins are a plausible answer to a real Kenyan problem, they carry issuer and redemption risk that cash does not, and the comparison that matters is against the actual cost of the actual alternative on your corridor.** Run that comparison with numbers rather than adopting a position.

### Part 4: What Regulation Does Not Fix

This is the section that matters most, because a licensing regime creates a dangerous illusion: that supervision equals safety.

**Volatility is untouched.** No regulator caps the price of anything. A licensed platform can list an asset that falls 80%, and the licence has nothing to say about it.

**Platform failure remains the central operational risk.** The history of this sector is a history of platforms that held customer assets and then could not produce them: through fraud, through lending out customer funds, through incompetent custody, or through simply disappearing. The general principle is set out in [the platform risk note](/insights/11-platform-risk), and it applies with unusual force here because of the next point.

**Custody is the question nobody asks.** If you hold assets on a platform, you generally hold a **claim against that platform**, not the asset itself. Whether you get it back depends on whether the platform segregated customer assets, whether it is solvent, and what the law says about your priority as a creditor. This is why "not your keys, not your coins" persists as a slogan; it is a crude statement of a real legal distinction. Self-custody moves the risk rather than removing it, because you then carry the risk of losing your own keys, which is permanent and uninsured.

**Irreversibility cuts the same way here as on RTGS.** A transfer sent to the wrong address is typically final. There is no reversal desk, no recall request, and no customer care line that can undo it. The discipline that [the wrong-payment guide](/blog/recover-wrong-payment-fraud-kenya) recommends for large bank transfers, independent verification before sending, applies more strictly, not less.

**Fraud adapts faster than rules.** Fake platforms, fake support staff, impersonated executives, phishing for seed phrases, and investment schemes promising fixed returns on digital assets are all common, and a genuine licensing regime gives them a new costume: claiming a licence they do not have. Verify claims on the regulator's own register, never on the provider's website.

**And a specifically Kenyan risk: concentration in a savings context.** Kenyan households have limited buffers. An asset class this volatile is not a substitute for an emergency fund, a Treasury bill or a pension, and sizing matters far more than selection. The framework in [how to evaluate any investment opportunity](/blog/evaluate-investment-opportunity-kenya) is the right screen to run before any of this.

> **Risk Factors:** Specific, current, and worth stating plainly. A provider that cannot tell you which regulator licenses it and under which Act. Any promise of fixed or guaranteed returns on a digital asset, which is the oldest structure in the book. Platforms trading on the VASP Act's existence as though it were an endorsement. Assuming the repeal of the Digital Asset Tax means digital assets are untaxed, when reporting obligations began on 1 July 2026 and the rest of the tax system never went away. Holding an emergency fund in a volatile asset. And self-custody without a tested backup and a plan for what happens to those keys if you die, which is a succession problem nobody thinks about until it is unsolvable, as [the wills and nominees guide](/blog/wills-nominees-business-continuity-kenya) implies for every asset with no registry behind it.

### Decision Framework: Before You Use Any Platform

**Which regulator, and is there a licence?** CMA for exchange, broking, advice, management and offerings. CBK for payments, custody and wallets. Ask for the licence, and check the regulator's own register rather than the platform's claim. Note that licensing is still being rolled out under the regulations, so an honest provider may say "applied, not yet granted", which is a different and more credible answer than a vague one.

**Where are the assets held, and in whose name?** Segregated customer custody or a pooled balance sheet claim. This determines what happens if the platform fails.

**What does it actually cost?** The 10% excise duty falls on platform fees, so the fee schedule is the number to interrogate, plus any spread on conversion into and out of shillings.

**What will KRA see, and what will I declare?** From 1 July 2026 platforms report on reportable users. Assume visibility and get your own tax position confirmed in writing.

**Is this money I can lose entirely?** If the answer is anything other than a clear yes, the position is too large.

**If I use self-custody, what is the recovery plan?** Including the version where you are not available to execute it.

### Bengula View

Three observations from the desk.

First, **the tax change was a genuine improvement and almost nobody noticed.** A 3% charge on transaction value is a brutal instrument: it taxes activity rather than profit, so a trader who ends the year down still pays it, repeatedly. Replacing it with a duty on platform fees taxes the service rather than the turnover, which is both fairer and more collectable. It is rare to be able to say a tax change made a market more honest, and this one did.

Second, **the reporting obligation is the real event of 2026, not the licensing.** Licensing determines who may operate. Information returns from 1 July 2026, plus a framework for automatic exchange with other jurisdictions, determine what the authority can see, and that is the change that alters behaviour. Anybody operating on the assumption that digital assets sit outside the system should treat that assumption as expired, and should sort out their position now rather than after a first request for information. The penalties fall on platforms, but the visibility falls on users.

Third, **the most useful thing this framework gives an ordinary Kenyan is a question, not a protection.** "Who licenses you, and under which Act?" is now a question with a correct answer, and the inability to give one is informative in a way it simply was not two years ago. That is a real gain for consumers. What it is not is a guarantee: a licensed platform can still list something that collapses, and supervision has never yet prevented a price from falling. Keep those two things apart in your head and this framework is genuinely useful. Confuse them and it becomes a new way to be reassured into a bad decision.

### Conclusion

Kenya now has a digital asset framework rather than a grey zone. The Virtual Asset Service Providers Act 2025 has been in force since 4 November 2025, the CMA supervises the investment-shaped activities and the CBK the payment-shaped ones, licensing is being switched on through regulations, and the transition window for providers already operating closes on 4 November 2026.

The tax position improved and then tightened. The 3% Digital Asset Tax on transaction value was repealed from 1 July 2025 and replaced with a 10% excise duty on platform fees, a much lighter and fairer base. Then from 1 July 2026 the Finance Act 2026 required platforms to file information returns to KRA on reportable users, and authorised international exchange of virtual asset information.

None of that makes any digital asset safe. Regulation reallocates accountability; it does not remove volatility, platform failure, custody risk or irreversibility. So use the framework for what it is genuinely good for: ask who licenses a provider and under which Act, find out where your assets are actually held and in whose name, assume your activity is visible to KRA, confirm your tax position in writing, and size any position at what you could lose completely without it changing your life.

### Frequently Asked Questions

**Is crypto legal in Kenya?** Virtual asset services are now regulated under the Virtual Asset Service Providers Act, 2025, in force since 4 November 2025. Providers require licensing under that framework, which is being implemented through regulations. Regulated is not the same as endorsed or guaranteed.

**Do I still pay the 3% Digital Asset Tax?** No. It was repealed with effect from 1 July 2025 and replaced by a 10% excise duty on the fees and commissions charged by virtual asset service providers. That is not the same as digital assets being untaxed; the rest of the tax system still applies to gains and income, and KRA's published position governs.

**Will KRA know about my crypto?** Increasingly, yes. From 1 July 2026 virtual asset service providers must file annual information returns to KRA on reportable users and controlling persons, with penalties for false statements, omissions and failure to file, and Kenya may enter agreements for automatic exchange of virtual asset information with other jurisdictions.

**Who regulates my exchange, the CBK or the CMA?** The CMA for exchanges, brokers, investment advisers, managers and offering providers. The CBK for payments, custody and wallets. Ask the provider which, and verify on the regulator's register.

**Are stablecoins a cheaper way to receive remittances?** Potentially, on some corridors, but a stablecoin is a claim on an issuer rather than cash, and the conversion to shillings at the destination carries its own cost. Compare end to end against the channels in the receiving-money guide before concluding anything.

**Does the European MiCA regime apply to me?** No. It is a useful comparison for how a mature framework handles disclosure and stablecoin reserves, and a platform's European compliance says nothing about its Kenyan licensing position.
