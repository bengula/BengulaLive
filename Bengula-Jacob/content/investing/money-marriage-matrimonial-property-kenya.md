Most Kenyan couples carry two contradictory beliefs about money in marriage at the same time. The first is that whatever is in your name is yours. The second is that, if the marriage ends, everything is split down the middle. Both are wrong, and the gap between them is where families lose homes, businesses and years in court.

The law is more specific than either belief. The Matrimonial Property Act 2013 decides what counts as matrimonial property in the first place. It ties ownership to each spouse's **contribution**, and it defines contribution to include work that never shows up in a bank statement. It presumes things about property held in one name or in two, and lets either spouse disprove them. It splits some debts equally. And it stops one spouse from quietly selling or mortgaging the matrimonial home. In January 2023 the Supreme Court settled the biggest open question: equality in marriage does not mean an automatic fifty-fifty split at divorce.

This article is about money during a marriage that is still going, and what happens to it if the marriage ends. Death and succession are a different body of law, covered in our pieces on [wills, nominees and business continuity](/blog/wills-nominees-business-continuity-kenya) and [land succession](/blog/land-succession-transmission-kenya). Here, both spouses are alive and the questions are practical. Whose name goes on the title? Should we open a joint account? Who is liable for the loan? What do I need to keep in case I ever have to prove what I put in?

> **Key Insight:** In Kenya, ownership of matrimonial property follows contribution, and contribution has to be proved. The Supreme Court held in 2023 that Article 45(3) of the Constitution does not entitle a spouse to half of everything on divorce. Each spouse must show what they contributed, in money or in kind. That makes record-keeping the single most valuable financial habit in a marriage. A title in one name does not end the argument, and a title in two names does not settle it either. The paper trail behind the asset does.

### Part 1: What Counts as Matrimonial Property

Not everything a married person owns is matrimonial property. Section 6 of the Matrimonial Property Act defines it as:

- the **matrimonial home or homes**;
- the **household goods and effects** in the matrimonial home or homes; and
- any other immovable and movable property **jointly owned and acquired during the subsistence of the marriage**.

That definition is narrower than most people assume. A plot one spouse bought alone before the wedding is not matrimonial property merely because the couple later married. Shares held by one spouse alone are not swept in automatically. What is swept in, whatever name is on the title, is the home the family lives in. Section 2 defines the **matrimonial home** as any property owned **or leased** by one or both spouses and occupied or used as the family home, including any attached property, so a rented family home counts too.

**Two things are carved out.** Section 5 provides that a person's interest in property **acquired or inherited before marriage** does not form part of matrimonial property. Section 6(2) excludes **trust property, including property held in trust under customary law**, which matters for family and clan land held by one spouse on behalf of others.

**Separate property can still give rise to a claim.** Section 13 confirms that marriage does not affect ownership of property other than matrimonial property. But under section 9, where one spouse owns property that does not become matrimonial property and the other spouse contributes towards **improving** it, for example by funding a house on land the first spouse already owned, the contributing spouse acquires a beneficial interest **equal to the contribution made**. Note the wording: equal to the contribution, not a share of the whole.

**Who the Act governs.** The Act covers marriages recognised in Kenyan law, but section 3 allows a person who professes the Islamic faith to be governed by **Islamic law** in all matters relating to matrimonial property. Polygamous marriages have specific rules in section 8: property acquired by the man and the first wife before he married another wife is retained equally by the two of them, while property acquired after a further marriage is treated as owned by the man and the wives taking each one's contribution into account, unless the parties agreed that a wife's property with the husband would be kept separate. Section 11 also requires customary law, including principles on ancestral land and the cultural home, to be taken into account on division, subject to the Constitution. If you are in a polygamous or customary union, take specific advice rather than relying on the general picture below.

**Cohabitation is not marriage.** A couple who live together without a marriage recognised in law are not covered by the Act as spouses. The Supreme Court in *MNK v POM* (2023) held that a presumption of marriage from long cohabitation can still arise, but only on strong evidence and sparingly. If you are building assets with a partner you have not married, the law of contract and trusts will govern a dispute, and your paper trail matters even more.

### Part 2: Contribution Is Broader Than Money

Section 7 vests ownership of matrimonial property in the spouses **according to the contribution of either spouse towards its acquisition**, and provides for it to be divided between them if the marriage is dissolved.

The definition of contribution in section 2 is what gives this teeth. It covers **monetary and non-monetary contribution**, and expressly includes:

- domestic work and management of the matrimonial home;
- child care;
- companionship;
- management of a family business or property; and
- farm work.

A spouse who did not pay a shilling towards the mortgage, but who ran the household, raised the children and managed the family shop while the other spouse earned the salary that paid the loan, has made a contribution the law counts. The Act also defines a **family business** as one run for the benefit of the family by either or both spouses, generating income or resources wholly or partly for the family. That is why a spouse who quietly kept the books for years has a claim on the business that a simple shareholding register does not show.

What the law does not do is tell a court how to price that contribution against a salary. That is the weighing exercise every contested case turns on, and it is why evidence matters.

### Part 3: The Supreme Court on Fifty-Fifty

Article 45(3) of the Constitution provides that parties to a marriage are entitled to equal rights at the time of the marriage, during the marriage and at its dissolution. For years, litigants read that as a right to half of everything.

The Supreme Court rejected that reading in *JOO v MBO* (Petition 11 of 2020, judgment 27 January 2023). The Court held that Article 45(3) guarantees **equality of rights**, not an automatic equal share of property. It does not entitle a court to vary existing property rights by itself. On dissolution, each spouse is entitled to a share that reflects their contribution, monetary and non-monetary, and that has to be proved on the evidence of each case. The Court also recognised that non-monetary contribution can be substantial, and that a court may conclude in a particular case that the contributions were equal.

Commentary since the judgment has not been unanimous. Some writers argue the case leaves the valuation of non-monetary contribution too open. But the practical lesson for anyone managing money in a marriage is clear. **Equal is an outcome you may have to prove, not one you are given.**

### Part 4: Whose Name Goes on the Title

Section 14 sets two rebuttable presumptions for property acquired during the marriage:

- property in the **name of one spouse** is presumed to be held **in trust for the other spouse**; and
- property in the **names of both spouses jointly** is presumed to be held in **equal beneficial shares**.

Rebuttable means either spouse can produce evidence to defeat the presumption. Courts have accepted bank records showing that one spouse alone funded a purchase as enough to rebut it. They have also held the joint-name presumption unrebutted where neither spouse could prove otherwise.

**Gifts between spouses** follow a different rule. Under section 15, property one spouse gives the other during the marriage is presumed, again rebuttably, to belong **absolutely to the recipient**. A car or a plot transferred to a spouse as a gift is, by default, theirs outright, not a shared asset waiting to be divided. If a transfer is not meant as a gift, say so in writing at the time.

The practical consequences of how you title property cut both ways.

| Your choice | What it gives you | What it does not give you |
|---|---|---|
| Title in both names | A presumption of equal shares, and both signatures needed to sell or charge | Protection from evidence that one spouse paid for it all |
| Title in one name | Control of the paperwork for the registered owner | Freedom from the other spouse's claim; the presumption runs the other way |
| Title in one name, contributions recorded | A clear evidential trail either way | A guaranteed outcome; the court still weighs non-monetary contribution |

**For banks and lenders,** the title is only the start. A lender taking a charge over property that may be a matrimonial home will ask about your marital status and, if you are married, require your spouse's consent (Part 6).

### Part 5: Joint Accounts, Shared Debt and Guarantees

**Joint accounts are about mandates, not just ownership.** A joint account comes with an operating instruction: either party may sign alone, or both must sign. An "either to sign" mandate is convenient and is also a single point of failure, because either spouse can empty it. A "both to sign" mandate protects both and makes routine payments slow. Many couples are better served by **two individual accounts plus one joint household account** with an agreed monthly transfer into it, so that household spending is shared, transparent and capped. What happens to a joint account on death is a succession question, covered in the [wills and nominees piece](/blog/wills-nominees-business-continuity-kenya).

**Some debt is shared by law.** Section 10 of the Act deals with liabilities. A liability a spouse incurred before the marriage and relating to a property remains that spouse's liability (section 10(1)), but if it was **reasonably and justifiably incurred** and the property becomes matrimonial property, it is shared equally unless the spouses agree otherwise (section 10(2)). Under section 10(3), spouses share equally any liability incurred during the marriage **for the benefit of the marriage**, and any reasonable and justifiable expense incurred for that benefit. Separately, section 16 provides that a spouse is not liable, solely by reason of marriage, for a **personal debt the other spouse contracted before the marriage**. In plain terms: a mortgage for the family home is a shared burden in a division, even if only one spouse signed the loan agreement. A personal car loan for a spouse's own use, or betting debts, sit far less comfortably under that wording.

**Being a co-borrower is different from being a spouse.** If you sign a loan as joint borrower, you are liable to the lender for the full amount regardless of what the Matrimonial Property Act says between you and your spouse. The Act shapes how the burden is shared between spouses. It does not reduce what the bank can recover from either of you.

**Guaranteeing your spouse's business loan** puts your own assets behind that business. If the business fails, the lender can pursue you as guarantor through the [enforcement ladder](/blog/debt-enforcement-ladder-kenya). Sign a guarantee as a financial decision, with a clear view of the business's accounts, not as a gesture of marital support.

### Part 6: Spousal Consent and the Matrimonial Home

Section 12 of the Matrimonial Property Act gives the home and other matrimonial property several layers of protection:

- **No disposal without both spouses (section 12(1)).** During a monogamous marriage, an interest in matrimonial property may not be alienated in any form, whether by sale, gift, lease, mortgage or otherwise, without the consent of both spouses.
- **The home specifically (section 12(5)).** The matrimonial home **shall not be mortgaged or leased without the written and informed consent of both spouses**. This subsection is not limited to monogamous marriages, and "informed" matters: a signature on a form the spouse did not understand is open to challenge.
- **A registrable interest (section 12(2)).** A spouse in a monogamous marriage, and in a polygamous marriage the man and any of his wives, has an interest in matrimonial property that can be protected by a **caveat or caution** on the title. A spouse who fears a sale behind their back can register one.
- **No eviction without a court order (section 12(3) and (4)).** A spouse cannot be evicted from the matrimonial home by the other spouse during the marriage except by court order. Third parties can evict only on a sale in execution of a decree, by a trustee in bankruptcy, or by a chargee exercising a power of sale. That last exception is why consent to a charge is so consequential.

Two land statutes reinforce the rule for the home itself. Section 79(3) of the **Land Act 2012** makes a charge over a matrimonial home valid only if it is executed by the chargor and any spouse living there, or the spouse has consented to it. Section 93 of the **Land Registration Act 2012** places the duty of making that inquiry on the lender. Courts have set charges aside where a lender failed to obtain consent, and the Court of Appeal has held that section 79(3) does not apply to charges created before the Land Act came into force.

**What this means in practice:**

- If you are borrowing against a home, expect the bank to ask for your spouse's written consent, or for a sworn statement that you are unmarried. The mortgage pieces on [KMRC loans](/blog/kmrc-affordable-housing-mortgage) and the [mortgage decision framework](/blog/mortgage-decision-framework-kenya) explain where this sits in the process.
- If your spouse is asked to sign a consent, they should read the facility, not just the consent form. Consent to a charge is consent to the home being sold if the loan defaults.
- A false declaration that you are unmarried, made to get a loan through without your spouse, exposes the transaction to challenge and you to serious legal consequences.

### Part 7: Prenuptial and Postnuptial Agreements

Section 6(3) allows the parties to an intended marriage to enter into an agreement before the marriage to determine their property rights. It is the statutory basis for prenuptial agreements in Kenya, and both section 7 (ownership by contribution) and section 13 (separate property) are expressly subject to such an agreement. Under section 6(4), either party may ask a court to set the agreement aside, and the court may do so if the agreement was influenced by fraud or coercion, or is manifestly unjust. The Act does not define those terms, so an agreement that produces a grossly one-sided result is the one most at risk.

A prenuptial agreement is most useful where one partner arrives with significant assets or a business, where either partner has children from a previous relationship, or where family land is involved. It is not romantic, and that is the point. It turns an argument you might have in court in fifteen years into a conversation you have now, while you are on the same side.

For property already held, the same purpose can be served by **recording contributions as you go** and by agreeing in writing how a specific asset is held, for example a written declaration of trust when one spouse funds a purchase registered in the other's name.

### Part 8: The Records That Win Arguments

Because contribution has to be proved, a marriage's paperwork is its insurance policy. Neither spouse needs to treat the other as an adversary to keep it. Keep it the way a business keeps its books, because one day someone may need to know who paid for what.

- **Bank and M-Pesa statements** showing deposits, instalments and transfers towards each significant asset.
- **Sale agreements, receipts and completion documents**, with the name of the person who paid.
- **Construction records** for any house: contracts, payments to the contractor, receipts for materials and who paid them.
- **Business records** showing who managed the family business, signed its cheques, kept its books and dealt with its customers.
- **Evidence of non-monetary contribution**: who ran the home, who looked after the children, who worked the farm. Courts accept witness evidence, but contemporaneous records carry more weight.
- **Loan documents** for any debt you claim was incurred for the marriage.

**You do not have to divorce to ask a court.** Section 17 allows a person to apply to court for a declaration of rights to any property contested with a spouse or former spouse, either within a matrimonial cause or on its own, even where no divorce petition has been filed. A spouse who believes property is being dealt with unfairly during the marriage has a route that does not require ending it.

> **Risk Factors:** The most common and most damaging risk is the absence of records: years of contribution that cannot be proved. The second is signing as co-borrower or guarantor on a spouse's facility without understanding that the lender can recover from you in full. The third is a home registered in one name, mortgaged without proper spousal consent, which exposes both the family and the lender to a dispute that can freeze the asset for years. Finally, transferring assets to relatives or into a trust while a matrimonial dispute is live invites the court to look through the transfer; our piece on [family trusts](/blog/family-trusts-holding-structures-kenya) explains why that rarely works.

### Bengula View

The Supreme Court's 2023 decision is good law for honest marriages and bad news for undocumented ones. It protects the spouse whose contribution was real but unpaid, because the Act counts domestic work, child care and the management of a family business. It does so only if that contribution can be shown.

From the banking side of the desk, the pattern we see most often is not conflict but drift. The house is in one name because that spouse had the payslip the bank wanted. The business is in one name because that is who went to the registrar. The joint account has an "either to sign" mandate because it was easier. None of those choices was made with the law in mind, and each of them shapes what happens later.

We would weigh two things most heavily. First, make titling and borrowing decisions deliberately and together, because they are the decisions the law will later read as evidence of intention. Second, keep the contributions file. It is the cheapest protection available to either spouse, and it serves the marriage as well as any dispute, because couples who can see where the money went argue about it less.

### Conclusion

Kenyan law does not split a marriage's wealth in half, and it does not leave it with whoever's name is on the paperwork. It looks at what each spouse put in, in money and in kind, and asks for proof. Decide titles on purpose, set up accounts with your eyes open, sign for debt only after reading it, and keep the records. The best financial protection in a marriage is a clear paper trail of a shared life.

### Frequently Asked Questions

**Is my spouse entitled to half of my property if we divorce?** Not automatically. Since *JOO v MBO* (2023), each spouse's share reflects proven contribution, monetary and non-monetary. Equal shares are possible, but they must be shown.

**Is land I bought or inherited before marriage matrimonial property?** Not by default; section 5 excludes it. But if it becomes the matrimonial home it is matrimonial property, and under section 9 a spouse who contributed to improving it, for example by funding the house built on it, acquires a beneficial interest equal to that contribution.

**Can my spouse sell or mortgage our home without my consent?** No. Section 12(1) bars alienating matrimonial property in a monogamous marriage without both spouses' consent, and section 12(5) bars mortgaging or leasing the matrimonial home without the written and informed consent of both spouses. You can also protect your interest with a caveat or caution on the title under section 12(2).

**Am I liable for my spouse's loan?** To the lender, only if you signed as borrower, co-borrower or guarantor. Between spouses, section 10 shares equally debts incurred for the benefit of the marriage, and section 16 says you are not liable merely because of the marriage for personal debts your spouse took on before it.

**Are prenuptial agreements valid in Kenya?** Yes. Section 6(3) allows parties to an intended marriage to agree their property rights before marrying, and section 6(4) lets a court set the agreement aside if it was influenced by fraud or coercion or is manifestly unjust. Have it drafted by an advocate.
