For decades, paying for university in Kenya meant one thing: applying for a HELB loan and hoping the allocation was enough to cover tuition and a modest upkeep. 

That system has fundamentally changed. The introduction of the New Higher Education Funding (NHEF) model shifted the burden of university costs, tying government support directly to household income through a complex means-testing system. For parents planning for their children's education, relying solely on state funding is no longer a viable strategy; a hybrid approach of state loans, scholarships, and private savings is required.

This guide explains how the current government funding mechanisms work, how HELB loans are priced and recovered, and how to use private education policies to cover the inevitable shortfall.

> **Key Insight:** An education policy from an insurance company is not an investment; it is a forced savings plan with a life insurance wrapper. It guarantees that the target amount will be paid out even if the parent passes away before the child reaches university age. If you only want return on capital, use a Treasury bond ladder. If you want certainty of the outcome regardless of mortality, use an education policy.

### Part 1: The Higher Education Loans Board (HELB)

HELB is a statutory body mandated to finance higher education for Kenyans. It provides loans (and some bursaries) to students in recognized universities and TVET institutions.

#### The Cost of HELB Money
HELB loans are highly subsidized. The interest rate is typically fixed at **4% per annum**. This is significantly below the rate of inflation and massively below commercial bank lending rates, making HELB the cheapest credit facility a Kenyan will likely ever access.

#### Repayment Triggers and Penalties
- **The Grace Period:** Historically, HELB granted a one-year grace period after graduation before repayment was due. 
- **The Penalty Trap:** Failing to begin repayment on time triggers aggressive statutory penalties (historically KES 5,000 per month of default). These penalties can rapidly eclipse the principal amount borrowed. 
- **Statutory Deduction:** Repayment is not optional. Employers are legally obligated to deduct HELB repayments directly from a loanee's salary and remit them to the Board, similar to PAYE or NSSF.

If you take a HELB loan, pay it. The clearance certificate is a mandatory requirement for public sector employment and is increasingly demanded by private sector employers. 

### Part 2: The New Higher Education Funding (NHEF) Model

Introduced to replace the older block-grant system, the NHEF model applies to students placed by KUCCPS (Kenya Universities and Colleges Central Placement Service) in public universities and TVETs. 

It is a **student-centered, means-tested** model. Instead of universally subsidizing university operations, the government funds the student directly based on their assessed level of need.

Students are categorized into bands (e.g., Band 1 for the most vulnerable, up to Band 5 for the least needy) using a Means Testing Instrument (MTI) that assesses household income, background, and family size.

**How it breaks down:**
- **Scholarships:** A direct government grant that does not need to be repaid.
- **Loans (HELB):** The repayable portion at 4% interest.
- **Household Contribution:** The cash amount the family must pay out of pocket.

A Band 1 student might receive 70% as a scholarship, 25% as a loan, and pay a 5% household contribution. A Band 5 student might receive a 30% scholarship, 30% loan, and face a 40% household contribution. 

Because the household contribution for higher bands can be substantial (often hundreds of thousands of shillings for technical degrees), parents must plan for this liquidity gap years in advance.

### Part 3: Private Education Policies

To bridge the gap between government funding and actual university costs, many Kenyan parents turn to education policies offered by life insurance companies.

As discussed in the [Endowment Plans Guide](/blog/endowment-plans-kenya-guide), an education policy is a specific type of endowment. You pay regular premiums over a fixed term (e.g., 10 or 15 years). At the end of the term, the policy pays out the sum assured plus accumulated bonuses.

**The primary benefit is the life cover.** If the premium payer passes away in year 3, the insurance company will waive all future premiums, keep the policy active, and still pay out the full intended university fund when the child turns 18. 

**The drawback is the yield.** Because a portion of your premium buys the life cover and administrative costs, the internal rate of return on the savings portion is lower than investing directly in a Money Market Fund or a Treasury Bond.

#### Alternative: The Bond Ladder
If you do not need the life insurance wrapper (perhaps you already hold a substantial standalone term life policy), you can fund education through a [Treasury Bond Ladder](/blog/fixed-income-kenya-complete-guide). 
1. Buy infrastructure bonds (IFBs) or long-tenor fixed coupon bonds matching your child's timeline.
2. Reinvest the semi-annual coupons into a Money Market Fund.
3. Liquidate or rely on maturity payouts when tuition falls due.

This yields a higher return on capital, but it requires discipline and carries the mortality risk: if you stop earning, the fund stops growing.

### Bengula View

Relying entirely on HELB and government scholarships is a strategy of hope. The NHEF model makes it clear that the state expects middle-income and upper-income households to bear a significant portion of higher education costs.

Plan for the household contribution early. If you lack the discipline to save, or if ensuring the fund exists even if you die is your primary concern, buy an education policy. If you have strict financial discipline and adequate standalone life cover, build a targeted bond and MMF portfolio. The only wrong choice is assuming the government will cover the bill.
