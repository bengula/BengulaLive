There is a specific conversation that happens in Kenyan families, usually at the worst possible time. Somebody needs money, for a medical bill, a business, a child's university place, and the obvious answer is the shamba. Then an advocate or a bank asks a simple question: **whose name is on the title?** And the answer is a parent who died in 2011.

At that moment the family discovers something it should have dealt with a decade earlier. The land is theirs in every sense that matters socially, and in no sense that matters legally. It cannot be sold. It cannot be charged to secure a loan. It cannot be subdivided among the heirs. It is, as [the property money guide](/blog/property-money-kenya-complete-guide) put it, economically frozen, and a very large amount of Kenyan household wealth is frozen in exactly this way.

This guide is about unfreezing it. It separates the three steps that families run together and then get stuck between, explains what the land registry actually requires, prices the delay honestly, covers the diaspora version where the heirs are in Dallas and the cousins are on the plot, and ends with the checks a **buyer** must run before paying for land caught in somebody else's unfinished estate. Because the buyer's risk here is just as real as the family's.

> **Key Insight:** Succession and transmission are not the same thing, and neither of them is the transfer. The court gives you authority over the estate. The land registry then puts the parcel into the personal representative's hands or straight into the beneficiaries'. Only after that can the parcel be dealt with normally. Families routinely get a confirmed grant, feel finished, and never go to the registry, which means the title still reads the way it always did and the parcel is still stuck.

### Part 1: Why the Title Cannot Move

The land registry is not being obstructive. It is applying a rule.

Under **section 61 of the Land Registration Act, 2012**, when a sole proprietor or a proprietor in common dies, the **personal representative may apply to be registered as proprietor on producing the grant to the Registrar**, and is then recorded as "executor of the will of" or "administrator of the estate of" the deceased. Subsection (2) offers a shortcut: once the grant has been confirmed and produced, the Registrar may register a transfer, or a surrender of a lease or discharge of a charge, by the personal representative **without requiring the personal representative to be registered first**. Subsection (3) defines "grant" as the grant of probate, the grant of letters of administration, or a grant of summary administration in favour of or issued by the Public Trustee.

Everything in that paragraph turns on one noun. **The registrar acts on a grant.** No grant, no dealing.

Behind it sits the rule from [the wills and nominees guide](/blog/wills-nominees-business-continuity-kenya): **section 45 of the Law of Succession Act** makes it an offence to intermeddle with a deceased person's free property without authority. So the family that "agreed among themselves" who takes which portion has done something socially sensible and legally weightless, and occasionally something worse than weightless.

One useful nuance. **Section 60 of the Land Registration Act deals with joint tenants**, and it works differently: on proof of death the Registrar simply deletes the deceased joint tenant's name by registering the death certificate. Survivorship does the work, and no grant is needed. That distinction between **joint tenancy** and **tenancy in common** is one of the most consequential choices two people ever make when registering a parcel together, and most make it without being told what it means.

### Part 2: The Three Steps, Separated

Here is where families lose years. These are three distinct processes, with three different institutions, and completing one does not complete the next.

| Step | Where it happens | What it produces | What it does not do |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Succession cause** | The court (High Court or a magistrate's court with jurisdiction) | A grant of probate or letters of administration, then a **confirmed** grant specifying the beneficiaries and their shares | It does **not** change the title. The register still shows the deceased. |
| **2. Transmission** | The land registry | The parcel registered to the personal representative as administrator or executor, or dealt with directly under s.61(2) | It does **not** give individual heirs marketable titles in their own names. |
| **3. Transfer** | The land registry | Titles in the beneficiaries' own names, or a transfer to a buyer | Nothing further, this is the end state. |

The most common failure is stopping after step one. A family fights through the succession cause, gets the confirmed grant, frames it, and assumes the land is now theirs. Years later someone tries to sell and finds the register unchanged. **The grant is authority; the register is ownership.** Both are needed.

The second most common failure is stopping after step two, with the parcel sitting in the administrator's name. That is legally clean but practically fragile: the heirs' shares depend on a confirmed grant and one person's cooperation, and if the administrator then dies, you have a new estate on top of the old one.

**On cost:** the confirmed grant specifies each beneficiary and their share, and the transfer to those beneficiaries is the point at which people brace for stamp duty. On KRA's published position, the **transfer of family property to members on the demise of the family member in whose name the property was registered is an exempted transaction**. That is a meaningful saving and it is the rule to work with. It applies to transmission to beneficiaries; it does not make a **sale** to a third party exempt, and the exemption has conditions and documentation, so have your advocate confirm the treatment for your specific parcel before budgeting.

### Part 3: The Timeline, and the Cost of Waiting

The statutory floor is fixed. **Section 71 of the Law of Succession Act bars confirmation of a grant until six months after it was issued**, unless the court directs otherwise because there are no dependants or the only dependants are adults who consent. Before that, the petition must be gazetted, with roughly thirty days for anyone to object. Before that, the family must assemble the death certificate, the identity documents, the chief's letter, the search, the list of assets and liabilities, and the consents of those entitled.

For an estate nobody contests, plan on **the better part of a year**. For a contested one, plan in years, and budget for it emotionally as well as financially.

The real cost, though, is not the filing fees or the advocate. It is what happens while you wait, and it compounds in three specific ways.

**Heirs multiply.** This is the big one. Every year a parcel sits unresolved, the chance rises that one of the original beneficiaries dies. When that happens, their share does not simply pass to their children informally; **their own estate must now be administered too**, and you are running two succession causes to solve one parcel. Leave it two generations and a straightforward matter involving four siblings becomes a matter involving nineteen people across three countries, several of whom have never met. Delay is not neutral. It is the single most expensive decision in this entire area.

**Evidence decays.** The people who knew which boundary was agreed, which portion was gifted in the 1980s, and where the original documents are kept, are exactly the people who die during the delay.

**Occupation hardens into a claim.** Under **section 7 of the Limitation of Actions Act**, an action to recover land cannot be brought after twelve years from when the right of action accrued, and **section 38** lets a person claiming title by adverse possession apply to the court to be registered as proprietor in place of the owner. Open, continuous, uninterrupted occupation over that period can therefore ripen into a legal claim. The family member or neighbour who has been farming the plot "while we sort out the succession" is, with enough years and the right facts, building a case. This is not a reason for paranoia about relatives. It is a reason to finish the process.

> **Risk Factors:** The specific things that turn a manageable estate into a decade-long dispute are predictable. A missing or contested list of beneficiaries. A second family nobody disclosed. A parcel already subdivided informally and occupied, with the register showing none of it. A confirmed grant that omits an asset, requiring a further application to rectify. An administrator who stops cooperating once the grant is in their name. And the quiet one: assuming the elder who "is handling it" actually filed anything.

### Part 4: Running It From Abroad

For diaspora families the problem has a second layer. The heirs are outside Kenya, the documents are inside Kenya, and the plot is occupied by people who are often also relatives.

**The power of attorney is the central instrument, and its limits are the point.** An attorney can sign, lodge and collect on your behalf. A **specific, time-limited power tied to the named parcel and the named transaction** is what you want: it should say whether the attorney may sign, lodge, pay, and receive documents, and it should expressly exclude borrowing, charging, selling, substituting another attorney, and varying terms, unless you genuinely intend otherwise. A general power of attorney handed to a relative in a difficult family situation is a risk you are creating yourself.

**The execution formalities matter and are easy to get wrong.** A power of attorney executed abroad must be **notarised and legalised** in the country of signing, and then **registered in Kenya**. The registration timing is the part people miss: a power of attorney must be registered within two months after signing, or, where it was signed outside Kenya, **within two months after it arrives in Kenya**. Stamp duty obligations also run on short clocks, generally within thirty days of execution. Have a Kenyan advocate confirm the exact requirements for your jurisdiction before you sign, because a defective instrument discovered at the registry costs another round trip of couriers and consular appointments.

**Occupation by relatives is the emotionally hard part and the legally urgent one.** Somebody is living on or farming the land. Very often that arrangement began as a family kindness and nobody wrote anything down. Two things are true at once: they may well have a moral claim that deserves acknowledgement, and, under the limitation rules above, prolonged undisturbed occupation can become a legal claim against the estate. The answer is not eviction by surprise. It is to start the succession promptly, document the arrangement honestly, and deal with the occupation as part of the distribution rather than pretending it is not happening.

**Practical controls for a family running this remotely:**

1. **Instruct the advocate directly**, in your own name, with a written scope and a fee agreement. Do not have a relative instruct them on your behalf and relay the news.
2. **Get the official search yourself** and read it. It shows the registered proprietor, the parcel details, and any encumbrances, charges, cautions or restrictions. A caution or restriction on the register tells you somebody else has already made a claim.
3. **Ask for copies of everything filed**, gazette notices included, rather than progress reports.
4. **Assume nothing about boundaries** until a surveyor has been on the ground.
5. **Keep the money trail clean.** Pay fees to the firm's account against an invoice, never in cash to an individual, and never to "speed things up" at an office.

### Part 5: If You Are the Buyer

This is the section that makes this a buyer-protection piece, because the person most exposed to an unfinished estate is often not the family at all. It is the outsider about to pay for the parcel.

The scenario is common. A seller offers land at a good price. The title is in the name of their late father. They explain, plausibly and often sincerely, that succession is "almost done" and that the family has agreed. They may have a confirmed grant. They may even have a letter from the chief. **None of that is enough.**

Run these checks, in this order, before any money moves.

1. **Get an official search on the parcel.** Read the registered proprietor's name. If it is a deceased person, stop treating this as a normal purchase.
2. **Demand the confirmed grant, not the grant.** An unconfirmed grant does not empower distribution of capital assets. A confirmed grant specifies each beneficiary and their share.
3. **Match the names.** The people signing the sale agreement must be the people named in the confirmed grant, or the personal representative acting in that capacity. A sibling selling "on behalf of the family" without that standing cannot give you good title.
4. **Check whether the transmission has happened.** If the register still shows the deceased, the transaction cannot complete until it does, and that is timing risk you must price, not absorb on a promise.
5. **Look for cautions and restrictions on the register.** These are the footprints of a dispute.
6. **Ask who is in occupation and go and see.** Occupation inconsistent with the seller's story is the single loudest warning sign available, and it costs a trip to check.
7. **Structure the payment against milestones**, with the balance held by an advocate and released on registration, never a large deposit against a promise to complete succession afterwards.
8. **Instruct your own advocate.** Not the seller's, not the family's, not the one who "already knows the file".

The general transaction stack, stamp duty at 4% urban and 2% rural on the higher of assessed value or price, the search, the valuation, the registration, is set out in [the property money guide](/blog/property-money-kenya-complete-guide). What this section adds is the one risk that guide flagged and handed on: **a seller who cannot give you clean title, however genuine their intentions, is not a seller yet.**

### Decision Framework: Where Your Family Actually Is

**Is the registered proprietor alive?** If yes, this is a normal transaction and the rest does not apply. Go and confirm rather than assume.

**Is there a grant, and is it confirmed?** No grant means start the succession cause now. An unconfirmed grant means you have authority but cannot yet distribute capital.

**Has the registry acted?** Get an official search. If the register still shows the deceased, step two is outstanding no matter what the court file says.

**Are any of the original beneficiaries now dead?** If so, you have more than one estate to administer and you need advice on sequencing before you file anything.

**Is somebody in occupation?** Then the clock in Part 3 is running, and the conversation cannot be deferred indefinitely.

**Are the decision-makers abroad?** Then get the power of attorney right at the start, specific and time-limited, rather than improvising it mid-process.

### Bengula View

Three observations from the desk.

First, **the cost of this problem is almost entirely a cost of delay, and delay is the one variable families control.** Nothing about a straightforward estate is inherently ruinous: the filing is routine, the six-month wait is statutory, the registry step is administrative. What turns it ruinous is ten years of "we will sort it after the funeral", during which beneficiaries die, memories fade, occupation hardens and the number of people who must now agree quietly triples. If you take one thing from this piece, take the sequencing: **start it while the people who know the facts are still alive to state them.**

Second, **the register is the only thing a bank or a buyer can act on, and families consistently underrate this.** A confirmed grant, a chief's letter, a family meeting and unanimous agreement among siblings are all real, and none of them is what a valuer, a lender's legal team or a purchaser's advocate looks at. They pull a search. If that search shows a dead person, the deal stops, regardless of how settled the family believes the matter to be. Finish at the registry, not at the court.

Third, **for diaspora families the honest risk is not fraud by strangers, it is drift among relatives.** The land does not usually get stolen. It gets farmed, then built on, then informally subdivided, in small reasonable steps that nobody recorded, by people who genuinely believed they were caretaking. Twelve years later the legal position and the human position have diverged badly and there is no clean answer left. The fix is unglamorous: instruct your own advocate, get your own search, write down the occupation arrangement, and finish the process while it is still only paperwork.

### Conclusion

A parcel titled to someone who died is not lost, but it is frozen, and it will stay frozen until three separate things happen: a court gives somebody authority over the estate, the land registry acts on that grant, and the parcel is then transferred into the names of those entitled. Families stall between step one and step two more often than anywhere else, and every year of stalling adds heirs, erodes evidence, and strengthens the claim of whoever is on the ground.

If your family has such a parcel, the instruction is simple and slightly unwelcome: start now, while it is a straightforward matter involving people who are still alive and still speaking to each other. If you are buying land from a family in this position, run the search, demand the confirmed grant, match the names, check the register, visit the plot, and hold the money until registration. And if your own title is clean, go back to [the wills and nominees guide](/blog/wills-nominees-business-continuity-kenya) and make sure your children never have to read this page.

### Frequently Asked Questions

**We have a confirmed grant. Is the land ours?** Not yet on the register. The grant gives authority; the transmission and transfer at the land registry are what change the title. Until the register is updated, the parcel cannot be sold or charged.

**Do we pay stamp duty when land passes to us as beneficiaries?** On KRA's published position, the transfer of family property to members on the demise of the family member in whose name it was registered is an exempted transaction. A subsequent sale to a third party is not. Confirm the treatment and documentation for your parcel with your advocate.

**The land is in Kajiado and my father's family follows customary rules. Does that change anything?** Possibly, substantially. Section 32 of the Law of Succession Act excludes agricultural land, crops and livestock in twelve listed districts, including Kajiado, and section 33 applies the community's own law or custom to their distribution. Take advice specific to the area.

**My uncle has farmed the plot since my father died in 2009. Can he claim it?** Prolonged open and uninterrupted occupation can found an adverse possession claim under the Limitation of Actions Act, where the facts support it. Whether it does here depends on evidence and is a matter for an advocate, urgently.

**Can I do all of this from abroad?** Yes, through a properly drawn, notarised, legalised and registered power of attorney, ideally specific to the parcel and the transaction and time-limited. Get it drafted by a Kenyan advocate before you sign anything abroad.

**Two of the original heirs have since died. What now?** Their shares form part of their own estates, each of which needs administering. This is exactly the compounding described above, and it needs professional sequencing rather than a family decision.
