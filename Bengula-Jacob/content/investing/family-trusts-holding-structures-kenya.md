Since 2021, the family trust has gone from a slightly exotic instrument in Kenya to something close to a fashion. It gets recommended at seminars, in WhatsApp groups and by people selling structures, usually with the same three promises: your assets will be protected, your family will avoid probate, and your wealth will pass down the generations intact.

The first promise is partly true, the second is mostly true, and the third is doing a great deal of work. What rarely gets said is the question that should come first: **is your estate actually complicated enough to need one?** For a great many families the honest answer is no, and what they need instead is the unglamorous package from [the wills and nominees guide](/blog/wills-nominees-business-continuity-kenya): a properly drafted will, updated nomination forms, and a second bank signatory.

This guide is the test. It covers what the 2021 reforms actually changed, what a registered family trust does and does not do, how it compares against an LLP and a holding company on the dimensions that matter, what the tax exemptions cover and precisely where they stop, what it costs to run, and the threshold questions that separate a family for whom this is transformative from one for whom it is expensive theatre.

> **Key Insight:** A trust works by separating ownership from benefit. You stop owning the asset; trustees hold it for beneficiaries under terms you set while you were still in a position to set them. That single mechanic is the source of every advantage a trust has, and of every disadvantage. It is why the asset does not sit in your estate and does not wait for a grant. It is also why you cannot simply change your mind, take it back, and treat it as yours again.

### Part 1: What the 2021 Reform Actually Changed

Before 2021, Kenyan trust law was workable but awkward for private families. The **Trustees (Perpetual Succession) (Amendment) Act, 2021** changed that in three ways that matter.

**It named the thing.** The Act introduces a statutory definition of a **family trust**: a trust, whether living or testamentary, partly charitable or non-charitable, that is registered or incorporated by any person or persons, jointly or individually, **for the purposes of planning and managing their personal estate**. Before this, private family arrangements sat uneasily in a regime built with charitable trusts in mind.

**It created a registration route.** Any person or group who have lawfully constituted themselves into a trust may apply to the **Principal Registrar of Documents** for registration. The Registrar has **60 days** to grant or reject the application, and a rejection must be given in writing with reasons. A granted application produces a certificate, and the trust takes on a legal existence separate from the individuals behind it.

**The Finance Act 2021 then made the tax treatment sensible.** This is the part that turned the structure from theoretically attractive to practically usable, and it is set out in Part 3.

The combined effect is that Kenya now has a recognisable, registrable, reasonably taxed private wealth vehicle. That is a real improvement. It is not, on its own, a reason for any particular family to use one.

### Part 2: What a Trust Does, and What It Does Not

Strip the marketing away and a properly constituted family trust does four things well.

**It takes assets out of your estate.** This is the headline benefit and it is genuine. Assets settled on a trust during your lifetime are not yours when you die, so they are not part of the estate, so they do not wait for a grant of representation, do not sit through the six-month statutory period before a grant can be confirmed, and do not appear in the distribution that [the wills guide](/blog/wills-nominees-business-continuity-kenya) describes. For a family whose problem is liquidity and delay, this is the single most valuable property of the structure.

**It holds an asset that should not be divided.** A family business, a building, a farm. Under intestacy or even under a will, these get split into shares among heirs, and the heirs then disagree about whether to sell. A trust holds the asset whole and distributes the income, which is why it is described as owning **the asset rather than the argument**.

**It survives the people who set it up.** Trustees change, beneficiaries change, the trust continues. That continuity is the answer to the compounding problem in [the land succession guide](/blog/land-succession-transmission-kenya), where each year of delay adds another dead beneficiary and another estate to administer.

**It lets you set terms while you are able to.** Provision for a minor, a dependant with a disability, a spouse, staged distribution at ages you choose. A will can do some of this; a trust does it better and for longer.

Now the honest column. **A trust does not do these things.**

**It does not defeat dependants automatically.** Part III of the Law of Succession Act allows a dependant to ask the court for reasonable provision out of the net estate where the disposition of the estate fails to make it. A structure built primarily to strand a spouse or a child is the sort that invites challenge, and the facts around its creation will be examined.

**It does not protect assets you transferred to escape a known problem.** Settling property on a trust while creditors are circling, or while a matrimonial dispute is live, or to put an asset beyond a liability you already had, is the fact pattern that gets attacked. Timing is everything: asset protection works where it is done early, for genuine reasons, long before a claim exists. It fails where it looks like flight.

**It does not let you keep control and also claim you gave it away.** This is the most common practical failure. A settlor who appoints themselves sole trustee, keeps all the powers, uses the assets exactly as before and treats the trust account as their own has created paperwork, not a structure. If the substance says the assets are still yours, do not be surprised when someone argues that.

**It does not fix bad records.** A trust holding a parcel whose title is still in a dead parent's name solves nothing. Finish the succession first; then consider the structure.

### Part 3: The Tax Position, and Exactly Where It Stops

The Finance Act 2021 amendments are why this structure became practical. The provisions to know:

| Treatment | Provision | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Transfer of property or shares **into** a registered family trust | Income Tax Act, First Schedule, Part I, paragraph 36(g) | Exempt |
| **Income or principal sum** of a registered family trust | Income Tax Act, First Schedule, Part I, paragraph 57 | Exempt |
| **Capital gains** on transfer of title to **immovable property** to a registered family trust | Income Tax Act, First Schedule, Part I, paragraph 58 | Exempt |
| Conveyance or transfer of property to a registered family trust | Stamp Duty Act, section 52(2)(b) | Exempt from stamp duty |

Two limits deserve emphasis, because they are where plans go wrong.

**The capital gains exemption is written around immovable property.** Shares are movable property, so a capital gain arising on transferring **shares** into a family trust does not attract the same immovable-property exemption. For a family whose principal asset is a company rather than a building, that is a material planning point and it needs to be modelled before anything is transferred, not after.

**The exemptions attach to a *registered* family trust.** The structure has to actually be registered, and it has to actually be a family trust within the statutory definition. An informal arrangement described as a trust in conversation gets none of this.

And a word on where the tax hierarchy sits. [KRA's published position](https://www.kra.go.ke/) governs the tax treatment of any structure, and its word is what a practitioner has to work to. Statute and any binding court or Tax Appeals Tribunal decision sit above commentary. Treat the table above as the starting point for a conversation with your advocate and tax adviser, and confirm the current treatment before you move an asset, because the details here have moved before and can move again. The wider tax picture for an estate and its income sits in [the tax, compliance and cash guide](/blog/tax-compliance-cash-kenya-guide) and [capital gains tax in Kenya](/blog/capital-gains-tax-kenya).

### Part 4: Trust, LLP, or Holding Company

Most families arrive assuming the choice is "trust or nothing". It is not. Three structures do overlapping work, and the right one depends on what you are actually solving for.

| | **Registered family trust** | **Limited liability partnership** | **Holding company** |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Primary job** | Hold family assets across generations and outside the estate | Hold an asset jointly with defined partners and clean exit rules | Own operating businesses and assets under one roof |
| **Who controls** | Trustees, under the trust deed | Partners, under the partnership agreement | Directors, answerable to shareholders |
| **Survives a death?** | Yes, that is the point | Yes, the LLP continues; the deceased's interest passes to their estate | Yes, the company continues; the **shares** pass into the estate |
| **Does it bypass the estate?** | Yes, for assets properly settled on it | No. The partnership interest is an estate asset | No. The shares are an estate asset |
| **Ongoing formality** | Trustee administration, accounts, meetings | Annual filings, partner accounts | Statutory filings, accounts, audit where required |
| **Best when** | Wealth must pass to and provide for people over time, or an asset must not be split | A defined group buys and holds an asset together | Businesses need consolidation, ring-fencing, and a clean corporate story |

Read across the "bypass the estate" row, because that is the distinction that decides most cases. **The LLP and the holding company do not solve the succession problem; they just change what passes through it.** With an LLP your heirs inherit a partnership interest, with a company they inherit shares, and either way that inheritance goes through the same grant process, the same six-month wait and the same potential dispute. Only the trust takes the asset out of the queue.

That is why the sensible answer is often **a combination rather than a choice**. The LLP structure that [the chama land banking guide](/blog/chama-llp-land-banking-kenya) sets out is the right vehicle for a group of unrelated people buying land together. A holding company is right when there are operating businesses to organise, and what those shares are worth is a question [the valuation guide](/blog/business-valuation-kenya-guide) answers. A trust sits above either of them, holding the partnership interest or the shares, when the objective is that the family's position survives the death of any individual member.

### Part 5: What It Costs, and the Honest Threshold

Nobody enjoys this part of the conversation, which is precisely why it belongs here.

A family trust has **set-up costs** (drafting the deed properly, which is a bespoke legal job and not a template exercise; registration; and the transfer of each asset into the trust, with its own conveyancing and registry work) and **ongoing costs** (trustee administration, separate accounts and records, tax filings, and either professional trustee fees or the very real time cost of doing it properly yourself). Add the cost of getting advice each time the family's circumstances change.

Rather than quote figures that will date, use the threshold test.

**A trust is likely worth it when at least two of these are true:**

1. There is a **single indivisible asset** that would be destroyed by being split among heirs, a working farm, a family business, a building generating the family's income.
2. There is a **beneficiary who cannot manage their own inheritance**: a minor, a dependant with a disability, or someone whose circumstances make a lump sum unwise.
3. The estate is **large or complex enough** that a frozen year would cause real damage, or spans several asset types and jurisdictions.
4. There is a **credible succession dispute risk**, because the family shape is complicated and you would rather set the terms yourself than have a court do it.
5. You genuinely intend to **give up personal ownership**, and can live with that.

**A trust is probably expensive theatre when:**

- Your estate is a home, a car, a SACCO balance and a pension. Every one of those is better handled by a will plus correct nomination forms, at a fraction of the cost.
- The real problem is an unfinished succession on a parcel of land. Fix that first, and you may find you are done.
- You want the protection but intend to keep using the assets exactly as before.
- You are doing it because someone at a seminar said wealthy families do.
- The motivation is to put assets beyond a creditor, a spouse or a tax liability that **already exists**.

> **Risk Factors:** The failures here are mostly failures of execution rather than concept. A deed drafted from a template that does not fit the family. Assets that were never actually transferred into the trust, so the structure is empty and the estate still owns everything. A settlor who is also the sole trustee and sole beneficiary in practice. Trustees who keep no accounts and hold no meetings. And a trust created in a hurry during a crisis, which is both the worst time to design one and the moment its motives look weakest.

### Decision Framework: Should You Build One

**What problem are you solving?** Say it in one sentence. If the sentence is "my family should not have to wait a year to access money", life cover and a will may solve it more cheaply. If it is "the farm must not be subdivided by my four children", that is a trust problem.

**Is there an indivisible asset or a vulnerable beneficiary?** These are the two clearest justifications. Without at least one, look hard at the cheaper options first.

**Can you actually give up ownership?** If the honest answer is no, stop here. A structure you keep controlling is not protection, it is paperwork with a fee.

**Are your titles and records clean?** A trust cannot hold what you cannot prove you own. Finish the succession, update the register, locate the documents.

**Are you doing this early, for its own sake?** Planning done years ahead of any claim is durable. Planning done in the middle of a dispute is an invitation.

**Have you priced the running cost against the estate?** The structure has to earn its annual cost. Below a certain size it simply cannot.

### Bengula View

Three things from the desk.

First, **the vehicle is being sold to a lot of families who do not need it.** The 2021 reforms were a genuine improvement and they produced a genuine wave of marketing. For the household whose estate is a home, a SACCO balance, a pension and a life policy, the right answer is a will, correct nomination forms and a second signatory on the business account, and that answer costs a tiny fraction of a trust while solving nearly all of the same pain. Buy the structure when the structure is the answer, not because the structure is impressive.

Second, **when a trust is right, it is usually because of an asset that must not be split or a person who must be looked after.** Those two facts, more than estate size, are what make the case. A modest family with a working farm that four children would liquidate, or with a child who cannot manage a lump sum, has a stronger case for a trust than a wealthier family whose assets are liquid, divisible and already governed by good nomination forms. Size is the weakest of the criteria and the one most often cited.

Third, **execution is where these structures die.** The most common defective trust is not badly drafted, it is empty: a deed exists, a certificate exists, and the assets were never actually transferred. The second most common is controlled: the settlor is trustee, beneficiary and decision-maker, and nothing about the substance changed. Both are discovered at the worst possible moment. If you build one, transfer the assets properly, appoint at least one trustee who will say no to you, keep the accounts, and hold the meetings. A trust is an ongoing discipline, not a document.

### Conclusion

Kenya's registered family trust is now a real and reasonably taxed instrument: a statutory definition, a registration route through the Principal Registrar with a 60-day decision window, exemption of the trust's income and principal, exemption of capital gains on transferring immovable property in, and exemption from stamp duty on the conveyance. For the right family it solves problems nothing else solves, chiefly keeping an indivisible asset whole, providing for someone who cannot provide for themselves, and taking wealth out of the queue that freezes an estate for a year.

For most families, though, the cheaper package does most of the work, and the honest question is not "should I have a trust" but "what exactly would a trust do that a will, a nomination form and a life policy cannot". Ask it in that order. If the answer is specific, build the structure properly and run it properly. If the answer is vague, you have just saved yourself a great deal of money.

### Frequently Asked Questions

**Does a family trust avoid succession entirely?** For assets properly settled on it during your lifetime, yes, those assets are not part of your estate and do not await a grant. Anything you still own personally at death goes through the normal process, so most families end up with both a trust and a will.

**Can I be a trustee of my own family trust?** Commonly yes, but being the *sole* trustee with unfettered control over assets you also benefit from undermines the separation the structure depends on. Appoint co-trustees and take advice on the powers you retain.

**Is transferring my company shares into a trust tax free?** The exemptions on transfers into a registered family trust are the ones listed above, and the capital gains exemption is framed around **immovable property**. Shares are movable property and are treated differently. Model this with a tax adviser before transferring anything, and confirm the current KRA position.

**Is an LLP a cheaper substitute?** For a group buying and holding an asset together, an LLP is often the better fit, and it is covered in the chama land-banking guide. But it does not take the asset out of your estate; your partnership interest still passes through succession.

**How long does registration take?** The Principal Registrar has 60 days to grant or reject an application, and a rejection must be in writing with reasons. Preparing the deed and transferring assets properly takes considerably longer than the registration itself.

**We are in the middle of a family dispute. Should we set one up now?** This is the worst moment to do it and the moment it is most likely to be challenged. Deal with the dispute, then plan.
