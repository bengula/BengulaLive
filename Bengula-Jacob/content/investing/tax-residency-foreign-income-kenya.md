The modern Kenyan workforce is increasingly borderless. Software developers in Nairobi write code for tech firms in San Francisco; consultants in Mombasa bill clients in London; and Kenyan diaspora in Texas buy Treasury bonds in Nairobi. 

The money crosses borders seamlessly, but tax law does not. The Kenya Revenue Authority (KRA) applies fundamentally different rules depending on one critical legal classification: **Tax Residency**. 

If you get your residency status wrong, you risk either paying tax in two jurisdictions simultaneously, or facing steep penalties from KRA for undeclared global income. This guide strips down the Income Tax Act to explain how KRA decides where you belong, and how they tax what you earn.

> **Key Insight:** Tax residency has nothing to do with citizenship or immigration status. You can be a Kenyan citizen and a non-resident for tax purposes, or a foreign national on a work permit and a Kenyan tax resident. Residency is determined almost entirely by how many days you physically spend inside Kenya.

### Part 1: The Tax Residency Tests

Under the Income Tax Act (Cap 470), the KRA taxes residents on their **worldwide income** (income derived from Kenya and income derived from outside Kenya). They tax non-residents **only on income derived from or accrued in Kenya**.

You are a tax resident of Kenya if you meet **any** of the following physical presence tests:

1. **The Permanent Home Test:** You have a permanent home in Kenya AND you were present in Kenya for any period in that year of income under consideration.
2. **The 183-Day Test:** You have no permanent home in Kenya, but you were present in Kenya for a period or periods amounting in aggregate to **183 days or more** in that year of income.
3. **The Averaging Test:** You were present in Kenya for any period in that year of income, AND in the two preceding years of income for periods averaging more than **122 days** in each year.

If you hit 183 days in a calendar year, you are a resident. If you hit 123 days every year for three years, you are a resident. Once you are a resident, your global income falls under KRA's purview.

### Part 2: Remote Work and Foreign Employers

This is the most common scenario for the modern Kenyan professional: living in Kenya, but working remotely as an independent contractor or employee for a company based in the US, UK, or Europe.

If you meet the residency tests (which you do, if you live in Kenya), **that foreign income is taxable in Kenya**. 

- **If you are an independent contractor:** You are running a business in Kenya. You must declare that income in your annual return, deduct allowable business expenses, and pay tax on the net profit at the standard graduated individual rates. You are also subject to instalment taxes if your liability exceeds a certain threshold.
- **If you are a direct employee (rare for remote foreign roles, but possible):** Since the foreign employer is unlikely to be registered for PAYE in Kenya, the burden falls entirely on you to declare the income and pay the appropriate tax.

Do not assume that because the money is paid in USD into a Payoneer or Wise account, it is invisible to KRA. Tax authorities increasingly share data globally, and transferring those funds into the Kenyan banking system leaves a clear trail.

### Part 3: The Diaspora and Non-Resident Taxation

If you are a Kenyan citizen living and working permanently abroad (e.g., in the US or UK), you likely do not meet the physical presence tests. You are a **non-resident** for tax purposes.

KRA will not tax your foreign salary. However, KRA **will** tax any income you generate *inside* Kenya. 

- **Rental Income:** If you own apartments in Nairobi and collect rent, that rent is taxable in Kenya (typically subject to withholding tax at source or declared via a non-resident return, depending on the structure).
- **Treasury Bonds and Dividends:** Interest from Kenyan Treasury bonds and dividends from NSE-listed shares are subject to Withholding Tax (WHT). For non-residents, WHT is usually a final tax.
- **Capital Gains:** If you sell land or property in Kenya, you must pay Capital Gains Tax (CGT) at the current rate (15% as of 2026), regardless of your residency status.

### Part 4: Double Taxation Agreements (DTAs)

The biggest risk of cross-border income is paying tax twice on the same shilling. 

For example, if you are a Kenyan resident consulting for a South African company, South Africa might deduct withholding tax on the payment before sending it to you. When you declare that income in Kenya, KRA will want to tax it again.

To prevent this, Kenya has signed **Double Taxation Agreements (DTAs)** with various countries (including the UK, South Africa, UAE, India, and several European nations). 

A DTA works in two ways:
1. **Exemption/Reduced Rates:** It may dictate that certain types of income are only taxed in one country, or cap the withholding tax rate the foreign country can apply.
2. **Foreign Tax Relief:** If you paid tax in the foreign country, KRA may allow you to offset that tax against your Kenyan tax liability on the same income, preventing double taxation. 

**Caution:** Kenya does *not* have a DTA in force with the United States. This creates significant complexity for US citizens residing in Kenya (who are taxed globally by the IRS based on citizenship) and Kenyans earning US-sourced income. Professional tax advice is mandatory in these corridors.

### Bengula View

Remote work has decoupled earning from location, but tax law is stubbornly geographically bound. The compliance burden for remote workers and diaspora investors is entirely self-directed; nobody is calculating PAYE on your behalf. 

Waiting for KRA to audit your bank deposits is an expensive strategy. The penalty for undeclared foreign income compounds quickly. Assess your residency status every January, track your days in-country if you travel frequently, and if you earn across borders, pay a qualified tax agent to file your return. The fee is lower than the penalties.
