Almost everything written about retirement in Kenya is about getting to retirement. Contribute here, claim this tax relief, choose between NSSF Tier II and a private plan, do not cash out when you change jobs. That advice is good, and [the retirement planning guide](/blog/retirement-planning-kenya-guide) covers it properly.

Then you arrive. There is a pot. And the questions change completely, into ones almost nobody prepares you for. How much can I take out each month without running out? Should I buy an annuity or manage the money myself? What happens to whatever is left when I die? And crucially: **how long do I have to make this last?** Nobody knows the answer to the last one, which is precisely what makes the decision hard.

This is a guide to the second half. It is not another explainer on pension contributions; for that, go back to the accumulation guides. This covers **decumulation**: the three risks you are actually managing, the tax position that changed materially in 2025, the three genuine routes to turning a pot into income, and what each of them leaves behind for the people who come after you.

> **Key Insight:** You are not choosing between products, you are choosing who carries the risk that you live a long time. An annuity transfers that risk to an insurer, permanently, in exchange for giving up the capital. Drawdown and a bond ladder keep the capital in your name and keep the risk with you. Everything else, the rates, the fees, the flexibility, is secondary to that single trade, and it is the trade most retirees never have explained to them.

### Part 1: What You Are Actually Deciding

Three risks, and every product is a different answer to them.

**Longevity risk** is the risk of outliving your money. It is not solved by being careful, because the problem is not knowing the number. Plan for twenty years and live twenty-five, and the last five are destitute. Plan for thirty-five and you will have under-spent a decade of your retirement for nothing. **Only an annuity actually removes this risk**, and it does so by pooling: the insurer takes money from everybody, pays everybody for as long as they live, and the people who die early fund the people who die late. You cannot replicate that on your own at any level of skill, because the mechanism is pooling, not investing.

**Sequence risk** is the one that surprises people who understand markets. Two retirees with identical average returns over twenty years can end up in completely different places depending on *when* the bad years arrived. If the poor returns land in years one to three, you are selling assets to live on while they are depressed, permanently shrinking the base that has to compound for the rest of your life. The same bad years arriving in year eighteen barely matter. This is why the early years of drawdown deserve more caution than the later ones.

**Inflation risk** is the quiet one. Kenyan inflation was **6.6% as at August 2026**. At that rate, purchasing power halves in roughly eleven years. A retiree who locks in a flat monthly income at 60 and lives to 85 will see that income lose most of its real value across the retirement it was supposed to fund. Any plan that ignores this is not a plan. The mechanics of nominal versus real returns are worked through properly in the inflation piece in this pillar.

A fourth consideration is not a risk but a preference, and it drives more decisions than people admit: **do you want to leave something behind?** That question has a different answer for every family, and it happens to be the question on which the three options differ most sharply.

### Part 2: The Tax Position Changed in 2025, and It Changed in Your Favour

This is the part of the decision most affected by recent law, and a lot of material still in circulation is out of date.

**Before 1 July 2025**, the Income Tax Act sheltered retirement benefits with monetary caps: the first KES 300,000 of annual pension or retirement annuity was exempt, lump sum withdrawals were exempt up to KES 600,000, and withdrawals on termination of employment were exempt up to the lesser of KES 60,000 per year of service or KES 600,000.

**The Finance Act 2025 deleted those monetary exemptions and replaced them with conditions.** Effective 1 July 2025, a payment from a registered pension fund, provident fund or individual retirement fund is exempt where:

1. the member **has attained retirement age** as defined in the rules of the fund or scheme; or
2. the withdrawal is made **before retirement age on grounds of ill health**; or
3. the withdrawal is made **after twenty years** from the date of registration as a member of the fund.

Read what that does. If you are retiring normally, **the cap is gone and your benefits are exempt**, not exempt up to KES 600,000. For a retiree with a substantial pot, that is a significant improvement over the old regime. The trade is at the other end: an early withdrawal that meets none of the three conditions no longer has the old monetary shelters to hide behind, which is deliberate, because the policy intent is to stop people cashing out their retirement savings when they change jobs.

Where a payment **is** taxable, the pension tax bands published by KRA apply: **10% on the first KES 400,000, 15% on the next KES 400,000, 20% on the next KES 400,000, and 25% above KES 1,200,000**. Separately, monthly or lump sum pension paid to a person aged **65 or over** has long been treated as exempt.

> **Risk Factors:** Some KRA and RBA guidance pages still display the older KES 600,000 lump sum and KES 300,000 annual figures, because published guidance lags statute. KRA's published position governs the tax treatment of your benefits, and where it conflicts with commentary, KRA wins. Confirm your own treatment with KRA or your scheme administrator before you draw anything, and get it in writing. The figures above are stated as at September 2026.

The wider compliance picture for a retired household's income sits in [the tax, compliance and cash guide](/blog/tax-compliance-cash-kenya-guide).

### Part 3: Option A, The Annuity

You hand your pot to an insurer. The insurer pays you an agreed income for the rest of your life.

**What it does well.** It is the only instrument that genuinely solves longevity risk. The payment continues whether you live five years or thirty-five. There is nothing to manage, no sequence risk, no decision to make each year, no temptation to overspend in a good year, and no exposure to your own future judgement, which matters more than people like to admit when planning for their eighties.

**What you give up.** The capital, generally and permanently. Once an annuity is purchased it is typically irreversible, and the money is no longer yours in any meaningful sense. That has a direct consequence for the estate, which Part 7 deals with.

**The variants that matter.** A **guaranteed period** annuity continues paying for a minimum term (commonly five or ten years) even if you die early, with the balance of that term going to your beneficiaries, which softens the worst case. A **joint life** annuity continues to a surviving spouse, usually at a reduced rate. An **escalating** annuity starts lower and rises by a fixed percentage each year, which is the only real answer to the inflation problem in Part 1. Every one of these features costs income today, and that is the honest trade: certainty and protection are bought with a lower starting payment.

**How to buy one properly.** Annuity rates vary between insurers, and they vary with your age, your gender, the guarantee, the escalation and prevailing interest rates. **Get quotes from several providers on identical terms**, and compare the actual monthly figure rather than a headline rate. This is one of the few genuinely irreversible financial decisions most people make, so treating it as a shopping exercise rather than an administrative step is worth real money. The general insurer-selection and claims-reliability points in [the insurance guide](/blog/insurance-kenya-complete-guide) apply here with unusual force, because you are relying on this counterparty for thirty years.

### Part 4: Option B, Regulated Income Drawdown

You keep the pot invested in an approved income drawdown fund and withdraw an income from it.

Kenya regulates this properly. Under the **Retirement Benefits (Income Drawdown Funds) Regulations, 2023** (Legal Notice 187 of 2023, effective 31 December 2023), the framework has three features you need to know:

- The **minimum drawdown period is ten years** from the date the drawdown commences.
- A member may withdraw an income subject to a **maximum of 12% per annum of the outstanding account balance**, reduced from the 15% allowed under the previous guidance.
- At the end of the minimum period the member decides whether to continue the drawdown, purchase an annuity, or take the balance as a lump sum, and a member continuing the drawdown may revisit the annuity choice periodically.

That 12% cap is a **ceiling, not a target**, and this is the single most misread number in Kenyan retirement planning. Drawing the maximum from day one is close to the worst thing you can do, because the percentage applies to the *outstanding* balance: as the balance falls, 12% of it falls too, and the income that felt comfortable at 60 is visibly shrinking by 70. The cap exists to stop the fund being emptied, not to tell you what is sustainable.

**What drawdown does well.** The capital stays yours. It stays invested, so it can grow. You keep flexibility to vary the income as circumstances change, and, crucially for many families, **whatever remains on your death is still an asset rather than an extinguished contract**.

**What it does not do.** It does not remove longevity risk. If you live a long time and markets are unkind early, you can run the fund down, and the ten-year minimum does not protect you from that. It is exposed to sequence risk in exactly the way Part 1 describes. And it requires either good ongoing judgement or good ongoing advice for twenty-plus years, including during the years when your capacity to exercise that judgement may be declining.

### Part 5: Option C, Your Own Bond Ladder

The third route is to take the benefits and build an income portfolio yourself, which in Kenya usually means government paper.

The attraction is specific and it is a tax attraction. **Infrastructure bonds are tax exempt**, which means the coupon you are quoted is the coupon you receive, with no withholding deduction, while a taxable instrument at a higher headline rate can pay you less. That arithmetic, along with the tax-equivalent yield calculation that proves it, is worked through fully in [the fixed income guide](/blog/fixed-income-kenya-complete-guide), and the portfolio construction, laddering across maturities so something matures regularly, is covered in [the monthly income engine](/blog/monthly-income-engine-kenya) and [the T-bill ladder guide](/blog/advanced-dhowcsd-t-bill-ladder).

**What this does well.** You keep complete control and complete ownership. The income is contractual rather than market-dependent, because a bond pays its coupon regardless of what prices do. The tax treatment on infrastructure paper is excellent. And the whole portfolio remains an estate asset.

**What it does not do, and this is the part enthusiasts skip.** It does not solve longevity risk either, and it adds three problems of its own. **Reinvestment risk**: your 12%-plus paper matures into whatever the market offers at that time, which may be far less. **Capacity risk**: this requires you to manage a portfolio competently into your eighties, and to have somebody able to take over if you cannot. **Concentration risk**: a portfolio entirely in government paper is a single-issuer bet, and while that issuer is the sovereign, "safe" is not the same as "riskless", a point [the sovereign debt explainer](/blog/sovereign-debt-explained) makes at length.

An illustrative comparison, on a **KES 10,000,000 pot**, purely to show the shape of the decision:

- **Drawdown at the full 12% cap**: KES 1,200,000 in year one, about KES 100,000 a month. But the cap bites on a falling balance, so unless returns match withdrawals the income declines year after year.
- **A tax-exempt infrastructure bond holding**: at a tax-free coupon of around 12.7%, roughly KES 1,270,000 a year, about KES 106,000 a month, **with the capital intact**, because you are spending the coupon rather than the principal.
- **An annuity**: the monthly figure depends entirely on your age, the guarantee, the escalation and the insurer's pricing, so it has to be quoted rather than estimated. What it buys that neither of the others buys is that the payment does not stop while you are alive.

Those numbers are illustrative and rate-sensitive, and the coupon used is indicative of recent infrastructure bond pricing rather than a current offer. The point is not the arithmetic. It is that **two of the three options look similar on the monthly figure and are completely different on who carries the risk of you living to 95.**

### Part 6: The Comparison

| | **Annuity** | **Income drawdown** | **Own bond ladder** |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Longevity risk** | Removed, insurer carries it | You carry it | You carry it |
| **Who manages it** | Insurer | Fund manager, with your decisions | You |
| **Sequence risk** | None | Significant, worst in early years | Low, if held to maturity |
| **Inflation protection** | Only if you buy an escalating annuity, and it costs income | Possible, depends on returns | Only if you can reinvest at higher rates |
| **Flexibility** | None once purchased | High, within the 12% cap | Complete |
| **Regulatory frame** | Insurance regulation | RBA, LN 187 of 2023 | None, it is your own portfolio |
| **On your death** | Stops, unless a guarantee or joint life applies | Balance remains, subject to scheme rules | Whole portfolio forms part of your estate |
| **Best suited to** | Anyone whose main fear is outliving the money | A pot large enough to absorb a bad decade | A confident investor with capacity and a successor |

The honest conclusion from that table is that for many retirees **the right answer is a combination rather than a winner**. Annuitise enough to cover the non-negotiable monthly costs, food, utilities, medical cover, so that those are guaranteed for life no matter what happens. Keep the rest in drawdown or in your own portfolio for flexibility, for the things you would like to do while you are well enough to do them, and for whatever you intend to leave behind. That split is not a compromise; it is the structure that matches each risk to the instrument that handles it best.

### Part 7: Leaving Money Behind

This is the arc that closes the cluster, and it is where the three options differ most.

**What each leaves.** A plain lifetime annuity leaves nothing; the contract ends when you do. A guaranteed period or joint life annuity leaves the balance of the guarantee, or a continuing income to a spouse. Income drawdown leaves the remaining fund balance. A bond ladder leaves the whole portfolio.

**And now the part that catches families out.** How that residue reaches your family depends on which instrument it sits in, and the routes are not the same.

- A **drawdown fund balance** is dealt with under the scheme rules and the retirement benefits framework. As [the wills and nominees guide](/blog/wills-nominees-business-continuity-kenya) sets out, a pension nomination is a strong indication and not an instruction: **trustees pay the nominated beneficiary but may refuse and record their reasons**, and where there is no nomination they distribute among dependants at their discretion. Name your beneficiaries, keep the nomination current, and make sure the trustees can see who actually depends on you.
- An **annuity with a continuing benefit** pays under the policy terms to whoever the policy names. That is a contract, and it is only as good as what the contract says, so read the death provisions before you buy, not after.
- A **bond ladder, an MMF holding or a bank balance** is your property. It goes into your estate. That means a grant of representation, the six-month wait before confirmation, and the whole process described in [the wills and nominees guide](/blog/wills-nominees-business-continuity-kenya). It is the most flexible option while you are alive and the slowest to reach your family when you are not.

That asymmetry is worth planning around deliberately. A retiree who wants both control and speed of transfer typically ends up combining instruments: a portfolio for control, life cover with a named beneficiary for immediate liquidity, and up-to-date nominations everywhere else. Where the estate is large, indivisible or complicated, the structural options are covered in [family trusts and holding structures](/blog/family-trusts-holding-structures-kenya), and where property is involved, [land succession](/blog/land-succession-transmission-kenya) is the step that must be finished before any of it works.

### Decision Framework: Building Your Retirement Income

**What are your non-negotiable monthly costs?** Food, housing, utilities, medical cover. This number is the floor, and the case for annuitising at least up to the floor is strong, because those costs do not stop if markets fall.

**How big is the pot relative to that floor?** If the floor consumes nearly all of it, flexibility is a luxury you cannot afford and guaranteed income should dominate. If the pot comfortably exceeds it, you have room for drawdown or your own portfolio.

**What is your health and family longevity picture?** It is an uncomfortable question and it is the single most relevant input to the annuity decision.

**Who takes over if you cannot manage it?** If the honest answer is nobody, that argues hard against a self-managed portfolio, whatever the yield advantage.

**Do you intend to leave capital behind?** If yes, a plain lifetime annuity works against you, and the residue routes in Part 7 should shape the mix.

**Have you got quotes?** Annuity pricing varies between insurers. Comparing several on identical terms is the highest-value hour in this entire decision.

**Are your nominations current?** Every scheme, every policy, today. This is the cheapest item on the list and the most commonly neglected.

### Bengula View

Three observations from the desk.

First, **the 12% drawdown cap is being read as a spending recommendation, and it is not one.** It is a regulatory maximum designed to stop a fund being emptied. Because it applies to the outstanding balance, drawing the maximum is self-consuming: the fund shrinks, and next year's 12% is 12% of less. Retirees who anchor on it are often visibly worse off by their early seventies and cannot work out why, having never exceeded a limit they were told was there to protect them. Decide your income from what the fund can actually sustain, then check it is under the cap, never the other way round.

Second, **the 2025 tax change is genuinely good news and almost nobody has been told.** Retiring normally now means your benefits are exempt rather than exempt up to KES 600,000, which for a reasonable pot is a material difference in what you actually receive. Meanwhile a great deal of guidance still in circulation, including on official sites, shows the old caps. If you are near retirement, this is worth confirming in writing with your administrator before you draw, because it may change the sequence in which you take your benefits.

Third, **the instinct to avoid annuities entirely is the most expensive piece of conventional wisdom in Kenyan retirement.** The objections are real: the capital is gone, the rates feel unexciting, the money does not pass to the children. But those objections all price the good outcome, where you die on schedule and the portfolio held up. The annuity is not bought for that scenario. It is bought for the one where you live to 94 and the markets had a bad decade in your sixties, and in that scenario it is the only thing standing between you and dependence on your children. A partial annuity covering the essential floor buys a specific and irreplaceable thing: the certainty that the lights stay on regardless. Buy enough of it to sleep, and manage the rest.

### Conclusion

Accumulation has a clear playbook and Kenyan savers increasingly follow it. Decumulation has no playbook at all, which is why so many retirees make the largest irreversible financial decision of their lives on the basis of whichever product was explained to them first.

The decision is simpler than it looks once you see what is actually being traded. An annuity transfers longevity risk to an insurer and takes your capital. Regulated drawdown keeps your capital, caps your withdrawals at 12% of the outstanding balance, requires a ten-year minimum, and leaves the longevity risk with you. Your own bond ladder gives you complete control, an excellent tax position on infrastructure paper, and the same longevity risk plus the obligation to manage it yourself into old age.

Most people should combine them: guarantee the floor, flex the rest. Then do the two pieces of administration that cost nothing and matter enormously, confirm your tax position in writing and bring every nomination form up to date, so that whatever you do leave behind reaches the people you meant it for without a year's delay.

### Frequently Asked Questions

**How much can I withdraw from an income drawdown fund?** Up to 12% per annum of your outstanding account balance under the 2023 regulations, with a minimum drawdown period of ten years. Treat the 12% as a ceiling rather than a plan; a sustainable rate is usually well below it.

**Is my pension lump sum taxed?** Since 1 July 2025 the monetary exemption caps were replaced with conditions. Payments are exempt where you have attained retirement age under the scheme rules, or withdraw early on ill health, or withdraw after twenty years of membership. Where a payment is taxable, KRA's pension bands apply. Confirm your own position with KRA in writing.

**Can I change my mind after buying an annuity?** Generally no. Annuity purchase is typically irreversible, which is why comparing quotes from several insurers on identical terms beforehand matters so much.

**What happens to my drawdown fund if I die?** The balance is dealt with under the scheme rules. Trustees pay nominated beneficiaries but retain a discretion to refuse and record reasons, and distribute among dependants where there is no nomination. Keep your nomination current.

**Can I do both an annuity and drawdown?** Yes, and for many retirees a combination is the right answer: annuitise enough to cover essential monthly costs, keep the balance flexible.

**Is a bond ladder better than a pension?** They answer different questions. A ladder can produce excellent tax-exempt income and keeps your capital, but it leaves you carrying the risk of living a long time, and it requires you to stay capable of managing it.
