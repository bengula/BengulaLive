A Kenyan investor hears that Dangote is building in East Africa, that the refinery is the largest single industrial project on the continent, and that ordinary Africans are being invited to own a piece of it. The next move feels obvious: open the app, search the ticker, buy.

Then nothing works. The ticker is not on the NSE. The CDS account that holds your Safaricom and your KCB cannot see it. The broker says they can "look into it". And somewhere in that gap, a lot of Kenyans either give up or wire money to someone who should not have been trusted with it.

The confusion is not the investor's fault. Three separate things are tangled together in that one search: **which Dangote company is actually listed**, **which exchange it trades on**, and **whether a Kenyan can reach that exchange at all**. Untangle them and the answer is a sequence, like most things in finance. It runs through Lagos, not Nairobi, and the hardest step is not buying the shares — it is getting the money back out.

This guide covers what is genuinely purchasable, why your existing account cannot do it, the three routes that exist, and the foreign exchange mechanics that decide whether any of this was a good idea.

> **Key Insight:** The share price is not your main risk. For a Kenyan buying on an African exchange outside the East African Community, the dominant risks are **currency** and **repatriation** — whether you can convert shillings in, and whether you can convert the proceeds back out at a rate and a time of your choosing. A stock can rise 40% in local currency and still lose you money in shillings. Price the FX leg before you price the company.

### Part 1: What You Are Actually Trying to Buy

"Dangote" is a conglomerate, not a security. Before anything else, separate the listed businesses from the private ones.

The group's publicly traded companies trade on the **Nigerian Exchange (NGX)** in Lagos, denominated in naira. The best known are **Dangote Cement plc** and **Dangote Sugar Refinery plc**. These are ordinary listed equities: they publish accounts, they pay dividends, and anyone with access to the NGX can buy them.

The **refinery** is the one most people are actually asking about, and as at October 2026 it is a different animal. The Lekki refinery sits inside the privately held Dangote Industries structure. There is no ticker for it, which means there is no share of it for a retail investor to buy — not in Lagos, not in Nairobi, not through any broker. A listing has been publicly discussed more than once, and it may well happen; a project of that size is a natural candidate for the market. But discussion is not a prospectus.

This distinction is the single most important thing in this article, because it is where money gets lost:

- If someone offers you **pre-IPO shares in the refinery**, treat that as a fraud until proven otherwise. Unlisted shares in a private foreign company, sold to a Kenyan retail investor over WhatsApp, with a promise of an imminent listing, is a textbook advance-fee setup.
- A real IPO is announced by the company and the exchange, carries a published prospectus, and is bought through a licensed broker. It is not allocated by a stranger with a bank account.

So the honest framing: today you can buy the **listed Dangote companies** on the NGX. If the refinery lists later, the machinery in Part 3 is exactly what you will need to participate — which is a decent reason to understand it now rather than during an allocation window.

### Part 2: Why Your CDS Account Cannot Do This

Kenyan shares settle through the **Central Depository and Settlement Corporation (CDSC)**. Your CDS account is a record in that system — if you have not opened one yet, [how to buy shares on the NSE](/blog/how-to-buy-shares-nse-kenya) covers that ground first, and it is the right place to start. It can hold securities the CDSC is set up to clear: companies listed on the Nairobi Securities Exchange, government paper, and shares **cross-listed** onto the NSE from elsewhere in East Africa.

Cross-listing is the mechanism people are unconsciously hoping for. When a company lists its existing shares on a second exchange, investors on that second exchange can buy them locally, in local currency, through their normal account. Within the East African Community this genuinely happens — there is a working history of cross-listings between the Nairobi, Uganda, Dar es Salaam and Rwanda exchanges, which is why a Nairobi investor can sometimes reach a Kampala or Dar company without leaving the NSE.

Nigeria is not in that arrangement. The Dangote companies are not cross-listed onto the NSE. There is no local line, no shilling price, and no CDSC entry — so there is nothing your existing broker can route an order to.

That is not a regulatory prohibition on you, and this is worth being precise about: Kenyan law does not stop a resident individual from owning foreign shares. The obstacle is **infrastructural**. You are not banned from the NGX; you simply are not connected to it. Fixing that means getting connected, which is Part 3.

### Part 3: The Three Routes

There are three ways a Kenyan actually ends up holding this exposure. They differ enormously in effort, cost and control.

| Route | What it gives you | What it costs you |
|---|---|---|
| **Direct NGX account** | The actual shares, in your name, with votes and dividends | Most paperwork; Nigerian broker onboarding; FX on both legs; you manage repatriation |
| **International broker** | The shares, if the broker happens to offer NGX | Simpler onboarding, but NGX access is genuinely rare on retail platforms — verify before assuming |
| **Fund or ETF exposure** | Indirect exposure, professionally held and custodied | No direct ownership; a management fee; the fund decides the weighting, not you |

**Route 1 — a Nigerian stockbroker.** Nigerian listed equities clear through the **Central Securities Clearing System (CSCS)**, Nigeria's depository. You open an account with a broker licensed by the NGX, which issues you a CSCS account much as a Kenyan broker issues you a CDS account. Expect standard cross-border onboarding: identity documents, proof of address, a bank reference, and source-of-funds questions, which are more searching for a non-resident than for a local.

**Route 2 — an international broker.** Some global platforms aggregate many exchanges, and if one of them offers the NGX this is much the easiest path. Be sceptical here rather than hopeful: African frontier exchanges are the first thing retail platforms drop, and a platform offering "African exposure" is often offering funds rather than local lines. Confirm the specific exchange before you fund anything.

**Route 3 — collective investment.** Africa-focused funds, frontier-market funds and some offshore-mandated unit trusts hold these names already, and a fund solves custody, FX and repatriation in one decision. You give up the ability to choose the company. If what you want is **exposure to African industrial growth** rather than specifically these shares, this is usually the better-engineered answer, and it is the route most Kenyan investors should take. See [unit trusts beyond money market funds](/blog/unit-trusts-beyond-mmfs-kenya) for how these vehicles are structured locally.

### Part 4: The Foreign Exchange Leg

This is the part that decides the outcome, and the part almost nobody researches first.

Buying a naira-denominated share with shillings involves two conversions and two moments of exposure:

$$
\text{KES} \rightarrow \text{USD} \rightarrow \text{NGN} \rightarrow \text{shares} \rightarrow \text{NGN} \rightarrow \text{USD} \rightarrow \text{KES}
$$

Every arrow is a spread, and the round trip is the real cost of the position. A 1.5% spread on each of four conversions is 6% gone before the company has done anything. Compare that with the all-in cost of holding a [dividend sleeve on the NSE](/blog/dividend-income-nse-kenya), where you pay one set of local costs and no currency spread at all.

Then there is the question that matters more than the spread: **can you get the money out, when you want, at a rate you did not have to accept?**

For foreign investors in Nigeria, the instrument that governs this is the **Certificate of Capital Importation (CCI)** — documentary evidence, issued by the receiving Nigerian bank, that your capital genuinely came in from abroad through official channels. The CCI is what supports later repatriation of capital, dividends and sale proceeds in foreign currency. The practical rule is unforgiving and worth stating plainly:

> If your money enters Nigeria without a CCI, you may be able to buy shares, but you have weakened your position for getting the proceeds back out in hard currency.

The paperwork on the way in is the asset. Insist on it, keep it, and confirm with the receiving bank exactly what has been issued in your name before you place a single order.

Naira liquidity and the official rate have both moved substantially in recent years, and a Kenyan investor should assume that converting a meaningful sum out is a process rather than a transaction. If you have moved money across borders before, the mechanics in [receiving money from abroad](/blog/receiving-money-from-abroad-kenya) will feel familiar — only slower, and in the opposite direction.

### Part 5: Tax — The Questions to Settle Before You Buy

Cross-border investment income is the area where confident internet answers do the most damage, so this section deliberately asks questions rather than quoting rates. Rates and treaty positions change, and yours depend on facts specific to you.

Establish these four, in writing, with a tax adviser before you commit capital:

1. **Nigerian withholding tax on the dividend.** Nigeria taxes dividends at source. Establish the current rate applied to a non-resident individual, and whether your broker or the registrar deducts it automatically.
2. **Whether any treaty relief is available to you.** Do not assume a Kenya–Nigeria double taxation agreement covers your position; confirm whether one is in force and applicable, because the answer changes the net dividend materially.
3. **Kenyan treatment of the foreign income.** How the dividend and any eventual gain are treated in your Kenyan return, and whether tax paid in Nigeria is creditable against a Kenyan liability or simply lost.
4. **The gain on exit.** Kenyan [capital gains tax](/blog/capital-gains-tax-kenya) is charged on property situated in Kenya, which is why a foreign-listed share sits in a different analysis — and why that analysis should be done before you buy, not when you sell.

One general principle survives every rate change: **tax paid twice on the same income is a planning failure, not a law of nature.** It is usually the result of nobody asking question two in time.

### Part 6: The Honest Verdict

For most Kenyan retail investors, buying individual shares on the NGX is not worth the friction. Not because the companies are bad — Dangote Cement is a serious industrial business — but because the structure around the trade eats the thesis. Four currency conversions, a foreign depository, a repatriation regime you do not control, and a tax question requiring professional advice is a great deal of machinery to assemble around one holding.

The route is worth it in three situations:

- You are deploying a **large enough amount** that fixed friction becomes a small percentage.
- You have a **specific, researched conviction** about that company that a diversified fund would dilute.
- You are **positioning for a future listing** and want the account, the CSCS entry and the CCI process already working rather than scrambling during an allocation window.

If none of those describe you, take the exposure through a fund and spend the time you just saved on the part of your portfolio where you have an edge. There is an argument, made at length in [the honest comparison of Kenyan asset classes](/blog/ultimate-guide-to-investing-in-kenya), that most investors are better served by owning fewer things properly.

And if you only remember one line from this guide, make it this one: **nobody legitimate will sell you unlisted shares in the refinery.** The listing, if it comes, will be announced by the company and the exchange, with a prospectus, through licensed brokers. Everything else is someone else's exit, dressed as your entry.

### Before You Send Money Anywhere

A short checklist, in order:

1. **Confirm the security exists and where.** Ticker, exchange, and the exact legal entity. "Dangote" is not enough.
2. **Confirm your route actually reaches that exchange.** Ask the platform to name the exchange, not the region.
3. **Confirm the broker's licence** with the Nigerian Exchange or the relevant regulator directly, not via a link someone sent you.
4. **Confirm the CCI will be issued** in your name, by the receiving bank, before you transfer.
5. **Price the full round trip** — both FX legs, both sets of commissions, the custody fee, the withholding tax.
6. **Get the tax position in writing** on the four questions in Part 5.
7. **Then, and only then,** decide whether a fund would have done the same job for less.

The order matters. Every step above is cheap to do before the transfer and expensive to discover afterwards.
