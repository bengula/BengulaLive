Nobody wants to spend an afternoon thinking about their own death. So the documents that would make that death manageable for the people left behind, a will, an updated nomination form, a note saying where the title deed is, go unsigned for decades. The cost of that avoidance is not emotional. It is financial, it is measurable, and it lands on a grieving family at the exact moment they have the least capacity to deal with it.

The uncomfortable fact is this: **if you do not decide how your estate is divided, the law has already decided for you**, and the default it applies is very unlikely to be what you imagined. Worse, some of your biggest assets may not be governed by your will at all, because a form you signed when you joined a SACCO in 2009 quietly overrides it.

This guide covers what actually happens. It sets out the intestacy default in the [Law of Succession Act](https://new.kenyalaw.org/akn/ke/act/1972/14/eng@2022-12-31) (Cap 160), what a will can and cannot do, why nominations on SACCOs, pensions and insurance behave in three genuinely different ways, what happens to business bank accounts on the day an owner dies, and the short continuity checklist for a one-owner SME. It is the household and business companion to [the property money guide](/blog/property-money-kenya-complete-guide), which flagged this problem and handed off.

> **Key Insight:** Your will does not govern your whole estate. Property you own passes under your will or, failing that, under the intestacy rules. But a validly nominated SACCO interest, a life policy with a named beneficiary, and a pension death benefit are each dealt with under their own statute, and two of the three can bypass your estate entirely. A family can therefore do everything right on the will and still watch the largest single payout go to a person named on a form nobody had looked at in fifteen years.

### Part 1: What Actually Happens On the Day

Three things start at once, and they are easy to confuse.

**The estate freezes.** Section 45 of the Law of Succession Act is blunt about it: except as authorised by the Act or by a grant of representation, **no person shall take possession of, dispose of, or otherwise intermeddle with any free property of a deceased person**. Doing so is an offence carrying a fine of up to KES 10,000 or up to a year's imprisonment, and it makes the intermeddler personally answerable to the rightful executor or administrator for the assets they touched. In practice that is why a bank will not simply hand over a balance to whoever arrives with a death certificate.

**Somebody must be appointed.** Nobody has authority over the estate until a court grants it. Where there is a valid will, the executor applies for a **grant of probate**. Where there is not, the people entitled apply for **letters of administration**. Until that grant issues, the estate is in limbo, and the family's practical problem is that life does not pause to wait for it.

**Some assets never enter the estate at all.** This is Part 4 below, and it is the part most families get wrong.

It is also worth knowing what the Act does *not* reach. Section 32 excludes **agricultural land and crops, and livestock**, in the districts listed in the Schedule: West Pokot, Turkana, Marsabit, Mandera, Wajir, Garissa, Tana River, Samburu, Isiolo, Narok, Kajiado and Lamu. Section 33 sends the distribution of that excluded property to **the law or custom of the deceased's community**. If your family land sits in one of those areas, the rules below are not the rules that will apply to it, and that is a conversation to have with an advocate rather than an assumption to carry.

### Part 2: The Intestacy Default, and Why It Surprises People

Die without a valid will and the Act distributes what you leave. Here is the default, stripped to its structure.

| Who survives you | What the Act does |
|---|---|
| Spouse and children (s.35) | Spouse takes the personal and household effects **absolutely**, plus a **life interest** in the whole residue. A widow's life interest **ends on her remarriage**. On her death or remarriage the residue goes to the children equally. |
| Spouse, no children (s.36) | Spouse takes the household effects absolutely, **the first KES 10,000 of the residue or 20% of it, whichever is greater**, and a life interest in the remainder. On death or remarriage, the remainder passes down the s.39 order of kin. |
| Children, no spouse (s.38) | The net estate is divided **equally among the children**. |
| Neither (s.39) | In order: father, then mother, then brothers and sisters (and the children of deceased siblings), then half-siblings, then relatives to the sixth degree. Failing all of them, **the State**. |
| Polygamous marriage (s.40) | The estate is first split **between houses by the number of children in each house, adding each surviving wife as one extra unit**, then distributed within each house under sections 35 to 38. |

Three features of that table cause most of the pain.

**A life interest is not ownership.** This is the one that shocks families. Under section 35 a surviving spouse does not inherit the house and the land; she or he gets the use of it for life, with the capital held for the children. Section 37 allows a sale of property subject to the life interest only with the consent of all co-trustees and all adult children, or with the court's consent, and **for immovable property the court's consent is always required**. So a widow who needs to sell a plot to fund a business or pay school fees must go back to court to do it. Many discover this only when a buyer's advocate asks for the grant.

**Remarriage ends a widow's interest.** The proviso to section 35(1) is explicit. Whatever one thinks of it, it is the law as written, and a family planning around it should know it exists.

**Minor children's shares are held in trust.** Section 41 puts a child's share in trust until they turn eighteen, or marry under that age in the case of a daughter. Nothing can be distributed to them before then.

And one procedural fact governs the calendar: under **section 71, a grant of representation cannot be confirmed until six months after it was issued**, unless the court directs otherwise because there is no dependant, or the only dependants are adults who consent. Capital cannot be distributed before confirmation. Add the roughly thirty-day window after the application is gazetted for objections, plus the time to assemble documents and get a hearing date, and a **straightforward, unopposed estate realistically runs the better part of a year**. A contested one runs for years. That is the true cost of dying intestate: not a fee, but a family's access to its own money, suspended.

### Part 3: What a Will Does, and What It Cannot Do

Section 5 gives every adult of sound mind **freedom of testation**: you may dispose of all or any of your free property by will, and you may do so by reference to any secular or religious law you choose. A female person has the same capacity as a male person. That freedom is the whole point of writing one.

**The formalities are not onerous.** Under section 11, a written will is valid if the testator signs it or affixes a mark (or someone signs at their direction and in their presence); the signature is placed so as to give effect to the writing as a will; and **two or more competent witnesses** attest it, each having seen the testator sign or having received a personal acknowledgement, and each signing in the testator's presence. The witnesses need not be present at the same time, and no particular form of attestation is required.

**Oral wills exist but are weak.** Section 9 allows one, but only if it is made before two or more competent witnesses **and the testator dies within three months of making it** (the exception being active service in the armed forces or merchant marine). Section 10 adds that where witnesses conflict about what was said, the oral will fails except so far as a competent independent witness proves its contents. An oral will is a deathbed instrument, not a plan.

**A will does not make you untouchable.** Part III of the Act lets a **dependant** apply to the court where the will, or the intestacy rules, or the two combined, fail to make reasonable provision for them, and the court may order whatever provision it thinks fit out of the net estate. Section 29 defines dependant broadly: a wife or wives, **former wife or wives**, and children, whether or not they were being maintained; and parents, step-parents, grandparents, grandchildren, step-children, children taken into the family as the deceased's own, and siblings and half-siblings, **where they were being maintained immediately before death**. Where the deceased was a woman, her husband counts if she was maintaining him.

The timing matters: under section 30, **no dependant application can be brought once the grant has been confirmed**. That cuts both ways. It gives an estate finality, and it puts a hard clock on anyone with a claim.

The honest summary is that a will gives you control over the shape of the distribution and, just as importantly, over *who administers it*. It does not let you disinherit dependants without the risk of a court revisiting the decision. Writing one anyway is still overwhelmingly worth it, because the alternative is not "my family will sort it out", it is the table in Part 2.

> **Risk Factors:** Do not download a template, fill it in and file it away believing the job is done. The common failures are mundane and fatal: witnesses who are also beneficiaries, an unsigned or undated amendment, a will nobody can find, a will that disposes of property the deceased did not actually own, and a will drafted years before a divorce, a remarriage, or three more children. Use an advocate, tell somebody trustworthy where the original is kept, and revisit it whenever your family or your assets change shape.

### Part 4: Nominations Are Not Wills, and They Do Not All Work the Same Way

This is the section that changes outcomes, because these are often the largest and fastest payouts in an estate, and they are governed by three different statutes that behave in three different ways.

| Instrument | Governing rule | Does it bypass the estate? |
|---|---|---|
| **SACCO share and deposit nomination** | Section 39 of the Co-operative Societies Act | **Yes, completely.** The nominated interest passes directly to the nominee with no grant of representation and no monetary ceiling. |
| **Life policy with a nominated beneficiary** | Section 111 of the Insurance Act (Cap 487); and where the policy is expressed for the benefit of a spouse or children, the Married Women's Property Act creates a trust | **Yes.** The money secured by the policy is paid to the nominee and does not form part of the estate. |
| **Pension or provident fund death benefit** | Retirement Benefits Act and the scheme regulations and rules | **No, not reliably.** The benefit is paid to the nominated beneficiary, but the **trustees may refuse and must record their reasons**, and where there is no nomination they distribute among dependants at their discretion. |

Read that middle column again, because the practical consequences are large.

**The SACCO nomination is the sharpest instrument most Kenyans will ever sign.** It moves the member's share and deposits straight to the named person, outside the estate, outside the will, and outside the six-month grant timetable. That is extraordinary power in a one-page form. It is also why [the SACCO membership guide](/blog/ultimate-guide-to-sacco-membership-kenya) treats a stale nomination as a live risk rather than an administrative detail. A nomination naming a parent, made at joining and never revisited through a marriage and four children, will be honoured exactly as written.

**A pension nomination is a strong wish, not an instruction.** This surprises people who assume it works like the SACCO form. Under the retirement benefits rules the trustees pay the nominated beneficiary, but they retain a discretion to refuse and to record why, and they exercise a discretion over dependants where no nomination exists. The reason is protective: it stops a member's stale or coerced nomination from stranding actual dependants. The effect for planning purposes is that you should **name beneficiaries and also make sure the trustees can see who genuinely depends on you**, because they may look past the form.

**A life policy sits outside the estate, which is exactly why it is the liquidity tool.** Everything else in Part 1 and Part 2 is frozen for months. A policy with a properly named beneficiary pays out on proof of death and a claim, without a grant. That single property is what makes life cover the practical answer to the question "what will my family live on between my death and the confirmation of the grant?" It is the same mechanism [the insurance stack guide](/blog/insurance-stack-kenya-life-stages) describes for household protection, applied to the succession gap.

The instruction from all three is one sentence: **go and read what your SACCO, your scheme administrator and your insurer actually hold on file for you, and update anything that no longer reflects your life.** It costs an afternoon. It is the highest-return afternoon in personal finance.

### Part 5: What Happens to the Bank Accounts

Here the legal form of the business decides everything, and most one-owner businesses have never thought about it.

**A sole proprietorship has no separate legal personality.** The business is the owner. Its bank account, however it is branded, is the owner's personal asset, and on death it falls into the estate along with everything else. The bank will stop operations on the account once it is notified of the death. Standing orders for rent, salaries and supplier payments stop with it. The account is released to a personal representative under a grant, on the estate's timetable, which is Part 2's timetable. Meanwhile the business has payroll on the 28th.

**A limited company is a separate legal person, and it does not die.** The company's account belongs to the company, not to the shareholder, so the company's death is not triggered by the owner's. What passes into the estate is **the shares**. But the account can still seize up for a practical reason: if the deceased was the sole signatory or sole director, there may be nobody left with authority to operate it or to appoint anyone who can. The legal separation only helps if the governance behind it was built.

**Joint accounts and mandates.** A joint account with survivorship, or a business account with more than one authorised signatory, is the difference between a business that keeps trading and one that stops on the day of the funeral. This is not exotic planning. It is a mandate form at the bank, and it is the single cheapest continuity measure available to a small business.

The general point is that the **legal wrapper you chose for the business determines whether it survives you**, and most owners chose that wrapper for tax or registration reasons years ago without ever considering this. If you are weighing it, [the business banking journey guide](/blog/new-business-banking-journey-kenya) covers how the account and mandate structure is actually set up, and [the valuation guide](/blog/business-valuation-kenya-guide) covers what the shares passing into your estate are actually worth, which is a question your family will need answered.

### Part 6: The One-Owner SME Continuity List

If a business depends on one person, continuity is not a document, it is a short list of decisions made in advance. This is the version that fits on one page.

1. **Fix the legal form deliberately.** Know whether you are a sole trader or a company, and know what that means for the account on the day you die. If the business is worth preserving, the company form plus a second director is the structure that preserves it.
2. **Add a second signatory or a joint mandate now.** With dual authorisation controls so it does not become a fraud risk. This is the one item on this list that works immediately and costs nothing.
3. **Name and update beneficiaries everywhere.** SACCO, pension scheme, every life policy, and any employer group cover. Write down where each one is held.
4. **Carry key-person cover if the business would fail without you.** A policy on the owner's life, payable so that the business can survive the gap, is the standard answer, and [the SME insurance guide](/blog/business-insurance-kenya-sme-guide) sets out how it sits alongside the rest of the cover a business needs.
5. **Write the will, and include the business.** Say who gets the shares, and say who you want to administer the estate. The second half is as valuable as the first.
6. **Build the document file.** Title deeds, share certificates, the company registration, loan agreements, insurance policies, bank mandates, supplier contracts, the list of debtors, and the passwords to the systems that run the business. A business whose records die with its owner is very hard to rescue even when the law allows it.
7. **Tell one trusted person where all of it is.** Every item above is worthless if nobody can find it.

> **Key Insight:** Almost everything on that list is an afternoon of administration, not a legal project. The reason it does not get done is not cost or complexity, it is that the task has no deadline until it has a very bad one.

### Decision Framework: Where to Start

**Do you have dependants?** If yes, the will is not optional. Section 26 means the court can intervene where provision is unreasonable, so making the provision yourself is both kinder and more durable.

**Where is your biggest single asset?** If it is a SACCO balance or a life policy, your nomination forms matter more than your will does. Start there.

**Is there property in the family already caught in an unfinished estate?** Land still titled to someone who died years ago cannot be sold or charged until succession completes. Deal with that before it compounds into another generation.

**Does a business depend on you alone?** Then the second signatory and the key-person cover are urgent in a way the will is not, because they are what keeps the lights on during the months the estate is frozen.

**When did you last look at the forms?** If the answer is "when I joined", assume they are wrong.

### Bengula View

Three things from the desk.

First, **the intestacy default is not a neutral fallback, it is an active choice with a specific outcome**, and the outcome surprises nearly everyone who encounters it. The life interest under section 35 is the clearest example: families assume a surviving spouse inherits the home, and instead find a trust, a set of co-trustees, and a requirement to go to court before immovable property can be sold. Nobody would design that for their own family. They just never got around to designing anything else.

Second, **the nomination forms are where the real money moves, and they are the least maintained documents in Kenyan personal finance.** A will gets drafted with an advocate and a certain amount of ceremony. A nomination gets signed in a queue at a SACCO office and forgotten. Yet the nomination often controls a larger sum, pays out faster, and, in the SACCO case, overrides the will entirely. If you do only one thing after reading this, do the afternoon of form-checking.

Third, **liquidity is the problem nobody plans for.** The estate is frozen for months by design; the family's expenses are not. Between the funeral, the school fees and the payroll of a business that has just lost its owner, the money is needed immediately and the law says wait. The two instruments that solve this are a life policy with a named beneficiary and a business account somebody else can still operate. Both are cheap, both are arranged in an afternoon, and neither requires anyone to predict when they will be needed.

### Conclusion

If you leave no will, the Law of Succession Act divides your estate for you: household effects to the spouse absolutely, a life interest in the rest, the capital to the children, the whole thing suspended until a grant is issued and then confirmed six months later. If you leave a will, you shape that outcome and choose who administers it, though dependants retain the right to ask a court for reasonable provision until the grant is confirmed.

But the will is only part of the picture. A SACCO nomination bypasses the estate entirely. A life policy pays outside it. A pension nomination guides trustees who are not bound by it. And the day you die, a sole trader's account stops while a properly governed company's account does not.

None of this requires wealth to matter. It requires a family, or a business, or a plot of land, which is to say it applies to almost everyone reading. Write the will, update the forms, add the second signatory, tell someone where the documents are. Then go back to not thinking about it.

### Frequently Asked Questions

**Is a handwritten will valid in Kenya?** A written will must be signed by the testator (or at their direction) and attested by two or more competent witnesses who saw the signing or received the testator's acknowledgement, each signing in the testator's presence. Handwriting is not the problem; the signing and attestation are where homemade wills fail. Have an advocate draft or at least review it.

**Can my will override my SACCO nomination?** No. A validly nominated share or interest passes to the nominee outside the estate, so it never reaches the will. Change the nomination form itself.

**How long does succession actually take?** For a straightforward, unopposed estate, plan for the better part of a year: document gathering, the petition, roughly thirty days for objections after gazettement, the grant, then a minimum of six months before confirmation under section 71. Contested estates take far longer.

**What if my family land is in Turkana or Kajiado?** Section 32 excludes agricultural land, crops and livestock in twelve listed districts from these intestacy rules, and section 33 applies the community's own law or custom instead. Take advice specific to that area.

**My spouse died years ago and the title is still in their name. Can I sell?** Not until succession is completed and the title transmitted. This is extremely common and gets harder, not easier, with time as heirs multiply and memories fade. Start the process before you need the money.
