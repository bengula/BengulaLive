"The bank wrote it off" is one of the most comforting sentences in Kenyan personal finance, and one of the least accurate. It is usually delivered with relief, sometimes years after the fact, by someone who believes a chapter has closed.

It has not closed. A write-off is an accounting decision the lender makes about its own balance sheet, for its own regulatory reasons, and it changes nothing about what you owe. The Central Bank's own guideline tells banks to keep written-off debts in **memorandum accounts** and to have those accounts audited periodically. The file does not leave the building. It moves shelves.

There is a second belief that causes at least as much damage, and it runs the other way: that an old debt eventually dies of natural causes. Something like that is true, but the mechanism is not what people think, it does not apply to every kind of claim, and there is a very ordinary act, one that people perform precisely because they are trying to be reasonable, that resets it to zero.

This article deals with endings. It sits after [the enforcement ladder](/blog/debt-enforcement-ladder-kenya) and [what a creditor cannot take](/blog/protected-property-attachment-kenya) in the same cluster, and it answers the question those two do not: how long does this follow me, and what would actually make it stop?

> **Key Insight:** Two clocks run on every debt and they are unrelated. The **bank's clock** governs classification and write-off, ends inside the lender's accounts, and does nothing to your obligation. The **law's clock** governs whether a claim can still be brought to court, and it bars the remedy without cancelling the debt. Confusing the two is how people relax at exactly the wrong moment, and how they revive a claim that was weeks from being unenforceable.

### Part 1: The Bank's Clock

Kenyan banks do not decide when a loan has gone bad by instinct. The Central Bank of Kenya's prudential guideline on risk classification sets the ladder, and it is mechanical.

| Classification | Trigger, term loans | What it signals |
|---|---|---|
| Normal | Repayments up to date | No weakness |
| Watch | Principal or interest unpaid **30 to 90 days** | Potential weakness, not yet past due in a serious sense |
| Substandard | Past due **more than 90 days**, less than 180 | Primary repayment source insufficient; the lender is now looking to security |
| Doubtful | Past due **more than 180 days** | Not well secured; full collection highly questionable |
| Loss | Past due **more than 360 days** | Uncollectible, or of so little value that treating it as a bankable asset is not warranted |

A loan more than 360 days past due may still be held at Doubtful rather than Loss if it is backed by realisable security, and a Doubtful loan whose security has been sold or written down to zero drops to Loss unless it is actively being collected, including through legal action.

Then the write-off itself. The guideline directs an institution to write a loan off its balance sheet when it loses control of the contractual rights, or when the loan is deemed uncollectible, or when there is no realistic prospect of recovery. It lists the usual triggers: inability to collect, the borrower's bankruptcy, or collection efforts being abandoned for any other reason. And it puts a deadline on it: facilities classified as Loss **should be written off within 180 days of that classification** if nothing is recovered in the meantime.

Two details in that guideline matter enormously to you and are never mentioned in the conversation where you hear the words "written off".

The first is the stated purpose. Write-offs exist so that the institution's accounts "accurately reflect the true and fair financial condition of the institutions". The exercise is about the bank's books. You are not a party to it and you are not being released from anything.

The second is what happens to the file afterwards. The guideline requires that write-off procedures be documented to minimise abuse, that the **ultimate authority for approval rests with the board of directors**, and that **the memorandum accounts be periodically audited**. A memorandum account is precisely a record of something removed from the balance sheet but still tracked. The debt is carried off the books and onto a list that somebody is still responsible for.

### Part 2: So What Does a Write-Off Actually Change?

For the lender: the asset comes off the balance sheet, the provisioning position changes, and the reported financial condition becomes accurate.

For you: nothing legally. The contract still exists. The amount is still owed. The lender can still demand payment, still sue, still obtain a decree, and still execute against your property through every route in [the enforcement ladder](/blog/debt-enforcement-ladder-kenya). A write-off is not a waiver, not a settlement and not a discharge. It is bookkeeping.

The confusion is understandable, because the two things sound identical in English and because bank staff sometimes use the language loosely. But they are opposites in effect. A **write-off** is the lender's unilateral accounting entry. A **discharge or release** is a legal act, usually contractual, in which the creditor gives up the claim. Only the second one ends anything. If somebody tells you a debt has been written off, the question to ask is whether the bank will confirm **in writing** that the debt is released and the account closed with no balance outstanding. Those are different sentences and only one of them protects you.

### Part 3: And It Can Be Sold

A written-off debt remains property of the lender, and property can be transferred. A file that has stopped being worth internal collection effort may be assigned to somebody whose entire business model is pursuing exactly such files.

This is why people receive demands from companies they have never dealt with, years after the original lender went quiet, and assume it must be a scam. Sometimes it is. Often it is an assignee of a real debt. The practical response is the same in both cases and it is not to pay anything: ask for proof of the assignment and a statement of account showing how the figure was arrived at, and check the dates in Part 4 before you engage further. Do not, under any circumstances, make a goodwill payment to buy time. Part 6 explains why that single act can be the most expensive thing you do.

On collection conduct, the boundaries are covered in [the enforcement ladder](/blog/debt-enforcement-ladder-kenya): there is no debt collectors statute in Kenya, and harassment or contacting your relatives is a data protection complaint to the ODPC rather than a banking one.

### Part 4: The Law's Clock

The Limitation of Actions Act, Cap 22, sets how long a claim can be brought. The periods that matter here:

| Claim | Period | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Actions founded on contract, which includes an ordinary loan | **Six years** from when the cause of action accrued | s.4(1) |
| Actions founded on tort | **Three years** | s.4(2) |
| An action brought **upon a judgment** | **Twelve years** from delivery of the judgment, or from the date of default where payment was directed at a certain date or at recurring periods | s.4(4) |
| Arrears of interest on a judgment debt | **Six years** from when the interest became due | s.4(4) |
| Actions to recover land | **Twelve years** | s.7 |

Note the asymmetry inside section 4(4). The judgment itself has a long tail of twelve years. The interest that accrues on it has a shorter one of six. A creditor sitting on an old decree may find the principal still enforceable while older interest is not.

The distinction between a contract claim and a judgment matters more than anything else in this table. An unpaid loan that has never been sued on is a contract claim with a six year clock. The moment the lender obtains a decree, the claim merges into that judgment and a twelve year clock starts instead. Going to court does not just win the creditor an enforcement toolkit, it buys them time.

### Part 5: What the Clock Kills, and What It Leaves Standing

Here Kenyan law does something precise that almost every popular explanation flattens.

For **land**, section 17 provides that at the expiration of the limitation period, the title of the person who could have brought the action is **extinguished**. The right itself dies. That is the basis of adverse possession.

For **movable property** in conversion or detention cases, section 6(2) does something similar: where the period expires and the owner has not recovered possession, their **title to the property is extinguished**.

For a **debt**, there is no equivalent. The Act says an action "may not be brought" after six years. It bars the remedy. It does not extinguish the obligation. The debt continues to exist as a debt; what disappears is the creditor's ability to enforce it through the courts.

That sounds like a distinction without a difference, and for most practical purposes it is. A creditor who cannot sue you has very little leverage. But it has two real consequences. A time-barred debt can still be reported and discussed, and it can still be paid, and if you pay it you cannot demand it back on the ground that it was unenforceable. More importantly, because the obligation survives, it remains capable of being **revived**. An extinguished title cannot be resurrected. A barred remedy can.

### Part 6: The Trap, and It Is a Kind One

This is the most important section in the article, and the behaviour it warns against is the behaviour of a reasonable, decent person trying to do the right thing.

Section 23(3) of the Limitation of Actions Act provides that where a right of action has accrued to recover **a debt or other liquidated pecuniary claim**, and the person liable **acknowledges the claim or makes any payment in respect of it**, the right of action accrues **on and not before the date of that acknowledgement or the last payment**.

Read plainly: the clock does not pause. It goes back to zero and starts again.

$$ \text{New expiry date} = \text{Date of last payment or signed acknowledgement} + 6 \text{ years} $$

A loan last paid in 2019 is close to unenforceable by 2026. If, in 2026, the borrower is contacted and sends KES 1,000 as a gesture of good faith, the six years restart from that payment and the claim is live until 2032. Nothing was gained by the gesture. Almost six years of protection were given away in a single M-Pesa transfer.

The formalities are in section 24, and they contain a genuinely useful asymmetry:

- An **acknowledgement must be in writing and signed** by the person making it (s.24(1)). A phone call does not count. An admission made in conversation does not count. A signed letter, or a signed repayment proposal, does.
- A **payment** carries no such formality. Any payment counts. There is no minimum, no signature required, nothing to sign at all.
- Either must be made to the creditor or their agent (s.24(2)), and may be made by the debtor's agent.

So the risk is asymmetric in an unhelpful direction: the act that requires the most deliberation, signing something, is the one people instinctively avoid, while the act that requires none, sending money, is the one they instinctively perform.

One refinement from the proviso to section 23(3), which matters for rent and for interest: a payment of part of the rent or interest due at any given time does not extend the period for claiming the remainder then due, but **a payment of interest is treated as a payment in respect of the principal debt**. Servicing interest on an old loan keeps the principal alive.

None of this is an argument for dishonesty. It is an argument for advice. If a debt is genuinely old and you are considering engaging with a demand, find out where you stand on the calendar **before** you send anything or sign anything. That is a short conversation with an advocate and it is one of the highest-return conversations available in Kenyan consumer finance.

> **Risk Factors:** Three moves destroy a limitation position, and all three feel constructive at the time. Making a token payment to show good faith. Signing a repayment proposal or an acknowledgement of debt, including as part of a negotiation that then collapses. And servicing interest on an old facility while leaving the principal untouched, which the statute expressly treats as a payment on the principal. If there is any prospect that a claim against you is near or past six years, take advice before responding to a demand at all.

### Part 7: What Actually Ends a Debt

Having established what does not end it, the honest list of what does:

**Payment in full.** Obviously, and get a written confirmation that the account is closed with a nil balance.

**A settlement the creditor agrees to.** A negotiated reduced sum, accepted in full and final settlement, recorded in writing and signed by the creditor. This is the ending people think a write-off is. Ask for it explicitly; a bank that has already written a facility off internally may have more room to agree one than you expect, because it is negotiating over a recovery rather than over an asset.

**The limitation period expiring without revival.** The remedy is barred and the creditor cannot sue. The obligation technically survives, but with no enforcement route.

**Discharge in bankruptcy.** The genuine legal reset, available through the insolvency route, with consequences of its own that deserve their own treatment. That is the next article in this cluster.

**Death, partly.** Debts do not follow heirs personally, but they are claims against the deceased's estate and reduce what is distributed, which is why they belong in the succession conversation in [wills, nominees and business continuity](/blog/wills-nominees-business-continuity-kenya).

What is conspicuously not on that list: a write-off, a CRB listing being cleared, the lender going quiet, or the passage of time in itself without the limitation rules being satisfied. On the credit bureau point specifically, a listing is a record and clearing it has no effect whatsoever on whether the underlying debt is owed. The process for correcting one is in [how to fix your CRB listing](/blog/how-to-fix-your-crb-listing-kenya).

### Decision Framework: Working Out Where You Stand

**When was the last payment, of any size, on this debt?** Not when the loan was taken, not when it was written off. The date of the last payment or last signed acknowledgement is the date that starts the six years.

**Has anyone obtained a decree?** If yes, you are on the twelve year clock in section 4(4), not the six year one, and Part 4 of [the enforcement ladder](/blog/debt-enforcement-ladder-kenya) describes what that unlocks.

**Are you being chased by the original lender or by somebody else?** If it is an assignee, ask for proof of the assignment and a full statement of account before discussing anything.

**Is the claim near six years old?** Then do nothing, pay nothing and sign nothing until you have taken advice. This is the one situation in this cluster where inaction is the correct move.

**Do you want the debt actually resolved rather than aged out?** Then negotiate a written full and final settlement. That is the ending with a document behind it.

### Bengula View

Three observations from the desk.

First, **the language does the damage.** "Written off" sounds like an ending because in ordinary English it is one. In a bank it means the opposite: the asset has been recognised as unrecoverable *for accounting purposes* and moved to a memorandum account that somebody audits. We have watched borrowers make financial decisions for years on the strength of a phrase they misheard. If you take one sentence from this article, take this one: ask for the release in writing, or assume there isn't one.

Second, **the limitation trap punishes decency, and that should bother us.** The person who ignores every letter for six years is protected. The person who feels guilty, answers the call and sends a thousand shillings loses almost six years of protection in an instant. That is the actual operation of section 23(3), and the asymmetry in section 24, which requires writing and a signature for an acknowledgement but accepts any payment at all, makes it worse. Nobody designed this to trap the conscientious, but that is who it catches.

Third, **the twelve year judgment clock is the quiet reason to defend a suit.** An undefended claim is not merely a loss on the merits, it converts a six year contract clock into a twelve year judgment clock and hands the creditor the full execution toolkit. The single most valuable act available to a borrower anywhere in this cluster is responding to a summons, and it remains the thing Kenyan borrowers most reliably fail to do.

### Conclusion

Two clocks, doing different jobs. The bank's clock runs from 30 days past due to Watch, through Substandard and Doubtful, to Loss at 360 days, and then to a write-off within 180 days of that. It ends in the lender's own accounts, with the file in a memorandum account and the board's approval on record. It ends nothing for you.

The law's clock runs six years on a loan, twelve on a judgment, six on arrears of interest, and it bars the remedy rather than cancelling the debt. It can be restarted by an acknowledgement in writing, or by any payment at all, however small.

The two endings that genuinely work are a written release from the creditor and a discharge in insolvency. Everything else is either bookkeeping or the passage of time, and the passage of time is easier to undo than anyone expects. Before you respond to a demand on an old debt, find out which clock you are on and how much of it has run. Then decide what to do, rather than doing the instinctive, generous, expensive thing first.

### Frequently Asked Questions

**The bank wrote off my loan. Do I still owe it?** Yes. A write-off is the lender's accounting entry, made for regulatory reporting. The Central Bank's guideline requires written-off debts to be kept in memorandum accounts that are periodically audited. The contract, and the debt, survive.

**How long before an old loan cannot be sued on?** Six years from when the cause of action accrued for an ordinary contract claim, under section 4(1) of the Limitation of Actions Act. Twelve years if the creditor already holds a judgment.

**I paid KES 500 on an old debt to buy time. What did that do?** Under section 23(3), it restarted the limitation period from the date of that payment. Any payment counts, and there is no minimum.

**Does admitting the debt on the phone restart the clock?** No. An acknowledgement must be in writing and signed by the person making it under section 24(1). A payment, however, needs no formality at all.

**Someone I have never heard of is demanding payment. Is it a scam?** It may be, or it may be an assignee of a written-off debt. Ask for proof of the assignment and a statement of account, and check the dates before you engage. Do not make a goodwill payment.

**If the claim is time-barred, is the debt cancelled?** Not technically. For land, section 17 extinguishes title; for a debt, the Act bars the action but the obligation survives, which is why it can be revived by a payment or a signed acknowledgement.
