The most frequent question borrowers ask when dealing with aggressive recovery tactics is: "Is there a law against this?" 

The confusion arises because the answer is both yes and no. **Kenya has no standalone Debt Collectors Act and no specific debt collector's licence.** There is no single regulatory body that oversees "debt collectors" as a unified profession. 

Because of this vacuum, many borrowers assume collectors can do whatever they want, and some collectors behave as if that were true. In reality, while there is no single Act, collection conduct is tightly bound by four separate instruments. Understanding which rulebook applies to your situation determines whether you have a valid complaint and, more importantly, exactly which regulator to send it to.

> **Key Insight:** A debt collector on the phone and an auctioneer at the gate are entirely different legal entities. A debt collector is an agent calling to ask for money before a court process. An auctioneer is a licensed professional executing a court decree or a statutory power of sale. Treating them as the same thing is how borrowers miss the deadlines that actually matter.

### Part 1: The Four Rulebooks That Bind Collectors

When a collector crosses the line from firmness into harassment, the remedy depends on who the underlying lender is and what exactly the collector did.

#### 1. The Data Protection Act, 2019 (ODPC)
This is the most powerful tool currently available against aggressive collection, and it is the one that actually generates fines. 
- **The Rule:** A collector cannot process the personal data of third parties (your relatives, your employer, your emergency contacts) to pursue your debt unless those parties explicitly consented to be guarantors. They cannot "shame" you by broadcasting your default.
- **The Regulator:** The Office of the Data Protection Commissioner (ODPC).
- **The Teeth:** The ODPC has shown consistent willingness to enforce this. They have levied fines in the millions of shillings against digital credit providers for harvesting contact lists and sending threatening messages to third parties. If a collector calls your aunt to tell her you defaulted, that is a data protection complaint.

#### 2. Central Bank of Kenya Digital Credit Providers Regulations, 2022
If the debt originates from a digital lender (a mobile app loan), the collection process is regulated by the CBK.
- **The Rule:** Regulation 18 explicitly prohibits a digital credit provider or its agents from using threat, violence, or other criminal means to harm the person, reputation or property of a customer. It prohibits obscene or profane language, making unauthorised or unsolicited calls to contacts, and improper debt shaming.
- **The Regulator:** The Central Bank of Kenya (CBK).
- **The Teeth:** The CBK can suspend or revoke the licence of a Digital Credit Provider whose agents violate these rules.

#### 3. Business Laws (Amendment) Act, 2024
This recent amendment extended the anti-shaming protections beyond just digital apps to cover a wider range of credit providers.
- **The Rule:** It formally outlaws debt shaming and aggressive, non-consensual contact tactics across the credit sector, harmonising the rules so that a non-deposit-taking microfinance institution cannot use tactics that a licensed DCP is banned from using.

#### 4. The Auctioneers Act (Cap 526)
This applies *only* when the person at the door is a licensed auctioneer, executing a warrant.
- **The Rule:** Auctioneers are officers of the court (or acting under statutory powers). Their conduct, fees, and timelines are heavily regulated.
- **The Regulator:** The Auctioneers Board.
- **The Teeth:** The Board can suspend or revoke an auctioneer's licence for misconduct, overcharging, or failing to follow the procedural rules of attachment.

### Part 2: Debt Collector vs. Auctioneer

The distinction is critical because their powers are completely different.

| Feature | Debt Collector | Licensed Auctioneer |
|---|---|---|
| **Status** | Agent of the creditor. | Licensed professional under the Auctioneers Act. |
| **Power to attach property** | **None.** A collector cannot seize goods, lock a shop, or tow a car. | **Yes**, but only if they hold a valid court warrant or are exercising a statutory power of sale (like a logbook loan). |
| **Stage of process** | Pre-litigation. They are trying to get you to pay so they don't have to sue. | Post-litigation (execution of a decree) or executing a secured charge. |
| **Regulation** | Bound by Data Protection and CBK rules. | Regulated by the Auctioneers Board. |

If someone calls themselves a "debt collector" and threatens to come to your house to take your TV tomorrow without a court order, they are making an empty threat. Only a licensed auctioneer with a valid warrant can attach property.

### Part 3: The Auctioneer's Process and Your Rights

When a legitimate auctioneer arrives, they are executing a decree (as detailed in the [Debt Enforcement Ladder](/blog/debt-enforcement-ladder-kenya)). They cannot simply back a lorry up to your door and empty the house. They must follow the Auctioneers Rules 1997.

**The Proclamation and Inventory (Rules 12 and 13):**
1. The auctioneer must enter the premises and prepare a **signed inventory** of the goods they intend to attach.
2. They must leave a copy of the **proclamation of attachment** with you or an adult at the premises.
3. This proclamation gives you **seven days** to pay the decree amount plus the auctioneer's costs.
4. During this seven-day period, the goods are technically attached (they are in the custody of the law), but they usually remain on your premises. You commit an offence if you sell, hide, or move them.

**The Sale:**
If you do not pay within the seven days, the auctioneer may remove the goods and advertise them for sale. The sale must be by public auction, and it must be advertised in a newspaper of national circulation.

**The Exemptions:**
As detailed in [What a Creditor Cannot Take](/blog/protected-property-attachment-kenya), the auctioneer cannot attach your clothes, bedding, cooking vessels, or the necessary tools of your trade. However, it is **your responsibility** to point out that an item is an exempt tool of trade at the time of proclamation. If they proclaim an exempt item, you must urgently apply to the court that issued the warrant to have the attachment lifted.

### Part 4: Handling the Call

If you are receiving calls from a debt collection agency:

1. **Verify the debt:** Ask for written proof of the debt, the original creditor, and the agency's mandate.
2. **Keep records:** If the calls are abusive, record them (with notice if required, though keeping a log of times and content is often enough) and save any threatening SMS or WhatsApp messages.
3. **Know your leverage:** The collector is calling because the creditor has not yet chosen to spend the money to sue you. You still have room to negotiate a payment plan.
4. **Report breaches:** If they contact your employer or relatives without those people having signed as guarantors, file a formal complaint with the ODPC and copy the collection agency. The threat of a KES 5 million data protection fine usually stops the calls immediately.

### Bengula View

The lack of a specific Debt Collectors Act is a gap in Kenyan law, but it is no longer a free pass for abuse. The ODPC and the CBK have stepped into the vacuum, and their enforcement actions over the last two years have fundamentally changed how digital and consumer debt is collected.

For the borrower, the rule is simple: negotiate with the collector on the debt, but do not tolerate data breaches or threats. A collector who resorts to debt shaming is violating the Data Protection Act, and you should use that regulatory shield. However, never mistake a collector's call for an auctioneer's proclamation. One is a request for money; the other is the legal seizure of your assets.
