The library's enforcement cluster follows money from the borrower's side of the desk: what happens after a missed instalment, what property is protected, how long a decree lasts. This article stands on the other side. It is for the business owner who has delivered goods or services, issued an invoice, followed up politely, followed up firmly, and is now deciding whether to chase, write off, or sue.

The answer is almost never to start in court. It is a sequence, and the earlier stages are cheap, fast, and resolve most disputes before a filing fee is paid. But the sequence has to be run properly, because every stage creates the evidential foundation for the next, and a claim that arrives in court without the trail often fails on procedure rather than on merit.

> **Key Insight:** A demand letter is not a threat. It is the cheapest, fastest step in the recovery sequence, and it works most often precisely because it signals that the creditor knows the sequence and is willing to walk it. The debtor who ignores a well-constructed demand letter is the debtor who needs the next rung, and the letter itself becomes exhibit one when you get there.

### Part 1: Before the Letter, the Invoice Trail

Debt recovery starts long before the debt is overdue. It starts with the paperwork that proves the debt exists.

To succeed in any forum, from a phone call to the High Court, you need to show that a supply was agreed, the goods or services were delivered, and the amount was due and not paid. That evidence chain is built from documents most SMEs create but do not preserve:

- A **purchase order or written agreement** (even a WhatsApp confirmation with the scope, price, and delivery date).
- A **delivery note signed by the customer** or their agent, or proof of service completion.
- A **valid tax invoice** issued through eTIMS, correctly addressed and matching the delivery.
- **Payment terms** stated on the invoice or in the agreement. Where terms are not stated, the law implies a reasonable period, but "reasonable" is a fact question that slows everything down.
- **Follow-up correspondence**: emails, SMS, WhatsApp messages. These are evidence. Save them.

If you are an SME reading this after the fact and you do not have most of the above, the claim is not dead, but it is weaker than it needs to be, and the first thing an advocate will ask for is exactly this file. Build the habit going forward. The [eTIMS guide](/blog/etims-kenya-sme-guide) and the [13-week cash forecast](/blog/13-week-cash-forecast-kenya-sme) are the operational pieces that sit underneath this one.

### Part 2: The Demand Letter

A demand letter is a formal written notice to the debtor stating the amount owed, the basis for the claim, and a deadline to pay, failing which further action will be taken. It is not a court document and does not require a lawyer, though one sent on an advocate's letterhead carries a different weight.

A well-constructed demand letter includes:

1. **Your identity and the debtor's identity.** Full legal names, not trading names only.
2. **The specific invoices or amounts outstanding.** Invoice numbers, dates, and amounts.
3. **The contractual or commercial basis.** What was supplied, when, and what the agreed terms were.
4. **A clear, specific deadline.** "Within 14 days of this letter" is standard. "Immediately" is vague and unenforceable in the sense that it gives the debtor room to argue they were not given reasonable time.
5. **A statement of intended action if not paid.** "We shall institute legal proceedings without further notice" is the standard closing.

**Do not:** threaten criminal prosecution for a civil debt. Do not circulate the demand to the debtor's customers, employees, or social media. That crosses from recovery into harassment and potentially into defamation. The Data Protection Act applies to you as a creditor just as it applies to a debt collector.

**Do:** send the letter by registered post or a traceable courier, and retain the dispatch proof. A demand letter that cannot be proved to have been sent has limited evidential value.

The cost of a demand letter on your own letterhead is postage. The cost of one through an advocate is typically KES 5,000 to KES 15,000, and it is often the most cost-effective legal spend an SME will ever make, because a significant proportion of disputes resolve at this stage.

### Part 3: The Small Claims Court

If the demand does not produce payment, the next decision is which forum. For the overwhelming majority of SME trade debts, the answer is the **Small Claims Court**.

The Small Claims Court Act, 2016 established a simplified, low-cost forum for claims not exceeding **KES 1,000,000** (s.12(3)). The Chief Justice may vary this limit by Gazette Notice, so confirm the current threshold before filing.

**What it handles:** monetary claims arising from contracts, tort, or disputes between a landlord and tenant (other than possession). This covers unpaid invoices, goods not delivered, services not rendered, defective goods, and simple debt.

**What it does not handle:** employment disputes, criminal matters, or claims exceeding the pecuniary limit.

**Why it matters for SMEs:**

| Feature | Small Claims Court | Magistrate's Court |
|---|---|---|
| Maximum claim | KES 1,000,000 | KES 20,000,000 (Chief Magistrate) |
| Advocates | **Not allowed** (parties represent themselves) | Allowed |
| Filing fee | Modest, scaled to claim size | Higher, plus advocate's fees |
| Procedure | Informal, inquisitorial (the adjudicator asks questions) | Formal pleadings, evidence-in-chief, cross-examination |
| Typical timeline | Weeks to a few months | Months to years |
| Appeal | Limited (to the High Court on points of law only) | Full appeal on fact and law |

The no-advocates rule is the defining feature. It levels the playing field between a sole trader and a company with a legal department, and it compresses timelines dramatically because neither side is waiting for counsel's diary. The adjudicator may admit any evidence they consider relevant, including WhatsApp screenshots, delivery notes, and M-Pesa transaction records, without the formal rules of evidence that govern higher courts.

**Filing:** you file a claim at the Small Claims Court registry in the jurisdiction where the defendant resides, carries on business, or where the cause of action arose. The claim form is simple and the registry staff assist with it. Attach your demand letter, invoices, delivery notes, and any correspondence.

**Hearing:** the adjudicator will attempt mediation first. If mediation fails, the matter proceeds to a hearing, typically on the same day or within a short adjournment. Bring your documents and your witness (if you have one). Explain clearly what you supplied, what was agreed, and what remains unpaid.

**Decree:** if the adjudicator finds in your favour, a decree issues. The decree is enforceable the same way as any other court decree, through [the enforcement ladder](/blog/debt-enforcement-ladder-kenya): attachment, garnishee, or sale.

### Part 4: Above KES 1,000,000, the Magistrate's and High Court Route

Where the claim exceeds the Small Claims Court limit, or where the dispute involves complex legal questions, the claim goes to the Magistrate's Court (up to KES 20,000,000, depending on the grade of magistrate) or the High Court (unlimited jurisdiction).

This is formal litigation. It requires pleadings (a plaint, a defence, possibly a counterclaim), discovery, witness statements, a hearing, and judgment. It takes longer and costs more, because you will usually need an advocate.

Two tools within this route deserve mention because they are underused by SMEs:

**Summary judgment (Order 36 of the Civil Procedure Rules).** Where the claim is based on a liquidated sum (a fixed, ascertained amount, such as an unpaid invoice) and the defendant has no real defence, the plaintiff can apply for judgment without a full trial. The application must be supported by an affidavit deposing to the facts, and the court will grant summary judgment unless the defendant demonstrates a triable issue. This collapses months of procedure into weeks for clear debts.

**Statutory demand (under the Insolvency Act 2015).** Where a company owes you more than **KES 250,000** and the debt is undisputed, you may serve a **statutory demand** requiring payment within **21 days**. If the company does not pay, does not secure or compound the debt, and does not apply to set aside the demand, the company is deemed unable to pay its debts, and you may petition for its **winding up**. This is not a collection tool in the ordinary sense. It is a nuclear option, and it works precisely because no solvent company allows a winding-up petition to be presented. The statutory demand, properly served on an undisputed debt, produces payment faster than almost any other mechanism.

The threshold for a statutory demand against an **individual** debtor (a bankruptcy petition) is **KES 500,000** under the Insolvency Act 2015.

**Caution:** a statutory demand served on a genuinely disputed debt will be set aside by the court, the creditor may be ordered to pay costs, and the tactic will have wasted time and money. Use it only where the debt is clear, documented, and undisputed.

### Part 5: The Cost-Benefit Calculation

Recovery is not free, and the decision to pursue or write off a debt is a financial one, not a moral one.

| Recovery route | Typical cost range | Typical timeline | Best for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Demand letter (own) | KES 0 to 500 | 14 to 30 days | All debts, as a first step |
| Demand letter (advocate) | KES 5,000 to 15,000 | 14 to 30 days | Debts above KES 50,000 or repeat defaulters |
| Small Claims Court | KES 1,000 to 5,000 (filing fees) | 1 to 3 months | Debts up to KES 1,000,000 |
| Magistrate's Court + advocate | KES 30,000 to 150,000+ | 6 to 24 months | Debts above KES 1,000,000 |
| Statutory demand | KES 10,000 to 30,000 (serving costs) | 21 days + | Undisputed company debts above KES 250,000 |

The honest arithmetic is that pursuing a KES 30,000 debt through a Magistrate's Court with an advocate will cost more than the debt. That is why the Small Claims Court and demand letters exist, and why the sequence matters. The cheapest step resolves most disputes. The expensive step exists for the ones it does not.

A separate calculation: the cost of **not** pursuing. An SME that develops a reputation for not collecting teaches every subsequent customer that invoices are optional. The demand letter, even where it does not produce payment, produces a signal that invoices in this business are real documents.

### Part 6: Limitation Periods for the Creditor

A right to sue does not last forever. The Limitation of Actions Act, Cap 22, sets the following periods relevant to trade debts:

| Type of action | Limitation period |
|---|---|
| Simple contract (including an unpaid invoice) | **6 years** from the date the cause of action accrued |
| Specialty (contract under seal, or a deed) | **12 years** |
| Action upon a judgment | **12 years** from the date the judgment was delivered |
| Interest on a judgment debt | **6 years** from the date it became due |

The six-year clock starts from the date payment became due, not from the date of the invoice. If your terms were "30 days from invoice date" and the invoice was dated 1 January 2021, the cause of action accrued on 31 January 2021, and the limitation period expires on 31 January 2027.

A **part payment or written acknowledgement** of the debt by the debtor restarts the clock (s.23 and s.24 of the Limitation of Actions Act). This is the mirror image of the risk discussed in [write-offs and limitation](/blog/written-off-debt-limitation-kenya), where a debtor inadvertently restarts time. Here, it is in your interest to obtain a written acknowledgement from the debtor if the limitation period is approaching.

### Risk Factors

- **The Small Claims Court limit may change.** KES 1,000,000 is stated as at September 2026. The Chief Justice may vary it by Gazette Notice. Confirm before filing.
- **Statutory demands are sharp instruments.** Misuse against a disputed debt exposes the creditor to costs and reputational damage. Do not use them as a first resort.
- **Court decrees are not cash.** Winning a decree means you proved the debt. Collecting it requires execution, which is the territory of [the enforcement ladder](/blog/debt-enforcement-ladder-kenya).
- **Your own compliance matters.** A supplier who has not filed VAT returns, has no eTIMS invoices, or cannot produce a delivery note is a supplier whose claim is harder to prove. The compliance hygiene in [eTIMS for SMEs](/blog/etims-kenya-sme-guide) and [VAT for SMEs](/blog/vat-for-smes-kenya) is also a receivables-protection measure.

### Bengula View

The SME cash crunch is almost never caused by a single bad debt. It is caused by a system that does not chase. The business delivers, invoices, and waits. When the money does not come, the owner chases informally, gives up, and funds the gap with an overdraft that costs interest every day the receivable sits unpaid.

The fix is not aggressive. It is procedural. A demand letter sent on day 31, a follow-up on day 45, and a Small Claims filing on day 60, properly documented from the point of delivery, will collect more money than a year of WhatsApp messages and goodwill. The cost is negligible compared to the [overdraft that never clears](/blog/overdraft-that-never-clears-kenya) that funds the gap.
