Someone needs two thousand shillings before Friday. They search for the loan code for their network, and the results are a wall of blogs, each confidently publishing a short string beginning with a star and ending with a hash.

Some of those strings are correct. Some are out of date. And some were put there by people who understand something important about how mobile money works: a USSD session asks for your PIN, and a person who can choose which session you open is most of the way to choosing where your money goes.

This is the one financial instruction that should never be taken from a search result. Not because the blogs are all malicious — most are just stale — but because the cost of being wrong is your wallet, and the correct source takes thirty seconds to reach.

So this guide does something slightly different from what you searched for. It explains **where a real code comes from**, and then it spends most of its length on the part that actually decides whether the loan was a good idea: what a mobile loan costs when you convert the fee into a rate, and what happens if you cannot repay on the day.

> **Key Insight:** A mobile loan's advertised fee is not its interest rate, and the difference is enormous. A "one-off facility fee" of 7.5% on money you hold for 30 days is not 7.5% a year — annualised it is about **91%**, and if you roll it over every month it compounds past **140%**. The fee is small in shillings because the term is short, and the term being short is exactly what makes the rate large. Lenders quote you the shilling figure; the rate is what you are actually paying.

### Part 1: Where a Real Code Comes From

There are exactly three sources worth using, and they are all free.

1. **The operator's own app.** Airtel Money and M-PESA both have official apps, and lending products appear inside them as menu items rather than as codes you have to type. This is the safest route because there is no string to mistype and no session to impersonate.
2. **The operator's own website.** Not an aggregator, not a comparison blog, not a link from an SMS — the domain belonging to the network itself, typed by you into the address bar.
3. **Customer care, on the number printed on your SIM pack or the operator's site.** Ask them to confirm the current code and the current product name.

And three sources that should be treated as hostile until proven otherwise:

- **An SMS telling you that you qualify**, containing a code or a link. Operators do send genuine marketing; the problem is that you cannot tell the genuine from the forged by reading it, so verify through source 1 or 2 before acting on either.
- **A blog post listing codes for every network.** Even the honest ones go stale, because products get renamed and retired.
- **A person in a WhatsApp group who can "help you get a bigger limit."** No legitimate product has a human intermediary who improves your limit.

One structural point worth internalising: **a real mobile loan is drawn from inside your own wallet, by you, with no third party involved.** The moment a human being is part of the process, you are not taking a loan from a licensed lender — you are in a transaction with a stranger.

If you are on Airtel, note that product availability on the second network has historically differed from Safaricom's line-up, and names change. That is precisely why this guide will not print a code or a product name: by the time you read this, the operator's own channel is the only thing guaranteed to be current. Confirm it there.

### Part 2: What You Are Actually Agreeing To

Mobile credit in Kenya is regulated. Digital credit providers must be licensed by the **Central Bank of Kenya**, and licensing brought real obligations: disclosure of the total cost of credit, limits on how and when you can be pursued, and rules on what may be done with your data. If a lender is not on the CBK's published register, that is the end of the conversation.

Within the licensed universe, three structures get called "loans" loosely, and they behave differently:

| Structure | How it works | Where the cost hides |
|---|---|---|
| **Overdraft on a wallet** | Completes a payment you lack the balance for; repaid automatically from your next inflow | Charged per day outstanding; cheap for one day, punishing if it sits |
| **Short-term loan** | A lump sum into the wallet, repayable in 30 days or so | A one-off facility fee, which annualises very high |
| **Salary advance** | Advance against documented monthly income | Usually the cheapest of the three, and the slowest to arrange |

On top of the fee itself, expect **excise duty on the fee** — Kenya levies excise duty on financial-service fees, so the number you repay is the fee plus duty, not the headline fee. Confirm the current rate, as it has moved more than once.

The other term that matters is what happens on day 31. Some products roll over and charge again; some escalate; some simply report you. Ask which, before you borrow, because this is the mechanism that turns a two-thousand-shilling convenience into a problem that outlasts the thing you bought.

### Part 3: The Arithmetic

This is the part no lender puts in the advert, and it is not difficult.

$$
\text{Annualised rate} = \frac{\text{Fee}}{\text{Amount borrowed}} \times \frac{365}{\text{Days held}}
$$

Take an illustrative case — the numbers below are a worked example, not any particular product's pricing. Borrow **KES 2,000** with a **7.5%** facility fee, repayable in **30 days**:

- Fee: KES 150. Plus excise duty on that fee.
- As a proportion: 150 ÷ 2,000 = **7.5%** for the month.
- Annualised: 0.075 × (365 ÷ 30) = **91%**… if you only count it once.

But the realistic case is not one loan. It is the same two thousand shillings borrowed, repaid on payday, and borrowed again the following month — because the gap that caused the first loan has not closed. Twelve of those in a year is **KES 1,800 in fees on a facility that was never more than KES 2,000 outstanding**. That is 90% of the principal, paid for the privilege of being a month early, every month.

Now the comparison that reframes it. A **check-off or salary loan** at a bank or SACCO, which is the cheapest unsecured credit most salaried Kenyans can access, is priced in the mid-teens annually. The mobile loan is not twice as expensive as the alternative. Depending on how often you roll it, it can be **five to ten times** as expensive.

$$
\text{Cost of the habit} = \text{Fee per cycle} \times \text{Cycles per year}
$$

That second formula is the one worth remembering, because mobile credit is almost never a single event. It is a subscription you did not realise you had taken out. The honest version of [every loan and every lender in Kenya](/blog/borrowing-money-in-kenya-guide) sets the full ladder out; [check-off and salary loans](/blog/check-off-salary-loans-kenya) covers the product most people skip past on their way to the wallet.

### Part 4: If You Need Money This Week

The mobile loan exists because it is instant, and nothing below is instant. But most people reaching for it have more time than they think — the bill is Friday, not now — and an hour of effort is worth a 90% rate.

In rough order of cost, cheapest first:

1. **Your SACCO**, if you belong to one. A deposit-backed advance is usually the cheapest money available to an ordinary Kenyan, and many SACCOs process small advances in a day or two.
2. **A salary advance through your employer.** Often free or near-free, and it does not touch your credit file. The barrier is asking, not cost.
3. **Check-off through a bank or SACCO**, deducted at source. Mid-teens annually, and it is the product a salaried borrower is most likely to be under-using.
4. **A bank overdraft**, if you hold a current account with income running through it.
5. **The mobile loan** — genuinely the right answer when the need is same-hour and small, and the wrong answer for anything recurring.

The test is simple: **is this a timing problem or an income problem?** Mobile credit solves timing, expensively but effectively. It cannot solve income, and used against an income problem it accelerates the thing it was meant to relieve.

### Part 5: If You Are Already Behind

Three things are true and worth knowing precisely, because the fear around this is usually worse than the law.

**You will be reported, and that is not the end.** Licensed digital lenders share repayment data with credit reference bureaus. A default is recorded; it is not a blacklist in the way people describe it, and it does not make you permanently unbankable.

**There are limits on how you may be pursued.** Collection conduct is regulated, and there are rules about contacting your relatives and employer, about hours, and about what may be said. A collector who threatens you or calls your family is likely breaching the rules that bind them, and that is reportable.

**The debt does not last forever.** There are statutory limitation periods on recovering a debt through the courts, and a loan written off in a lender's accounts is not the same thing as a loan you no longer owe — the distinction catches people out in both directions. [What "written off" actually means](/blog/written-off-debt-limitation-kenya) covers it, and [the debt enforcement ladder](/blog/debt-enforcement-ladder-kenya) sets out what a creditor can and cannot do at each stage, from first demand through to attachment.

### Before You Dial Anything

1. **Get the code or the menu from the operator**, not from this page and not from a search result.
2. **Confirm the lender is CBK-licensed.** The register is public.
3. **Ask for the total cost of credit in shillings** — fee plus duty — before you accept.
4. **Annualise it** with the formula in Part 3. Thirty seconds.
5. **Ask what happens on day 31.** Rollover, escalation, or report.
6. **Ask whether a salary advance or SACCO advance would arrive in time.** Usually the answer is yes.
7. **Decide whether this is timing or income.** Be honest; it is the only question that matters.

*Rates, fees, excise duty, product names and USSD codes all change, and this guide deliberately prints none of them. Verify the current position with your operator and the Central Bank of Kenya's register of licensed digital credit providers before borrowing.
