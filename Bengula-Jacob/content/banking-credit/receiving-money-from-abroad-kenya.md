Diaspora remittances are one of Kenya's largest and most reliable sources of foreign exchange, running into billions of US dollars every year and, for millions of households, the money that pays school fees, builds a house, and funds a business back home. The [payment rails guide](/blog/how-to-move-money-kenya-payment-rails) covered sending money *out* of the country. This is the reverse and far more common flow for most Kenyan families: money coming *in* from a relative abroad.

And here is the quiet problem. Most people, sender and receiver alike, judge a transfer by its advertised fee, "send for only $3", and never look at the exchange rate applied. Yet the rate is where most of the cost lives. A provider can advertise a tiny fee and still take a larger cut than a "pricier" competitor, simply by giving you fewer shillings per dollar. Over a year of monthly transfers, the difference between the cheapest and the most expensive route is real money that stayed abroad for no reason.

This guide compares the ways money enters Kenya on the only measure that matters, **how many shillings actually land**, and then covers where the money should arrive, the tax question every receiving family asks, and the scams that specifically target the diaspora and their relatives at home.

> **Key Insight:** The fee is the small part; the exchange rate is the cost. To compare two ways of sending money home, ignore the advertised fee and ask one question: after everything, how many Kenyan shillings will actually reach the recipient? The provider that lands the most shillings wins, whatever its fee says, and the only way to know is to compare the all-in result, not the headline.

### Part 1: The Ways Money Comes In

Money reaches Kenya from abroad through three broad channels, and they trade off cost, speed, and how much they can carry.

**Bank telegraphic transfer (TT / SWIFT).** A wire from the sender's overseas bank into a Kenyan bank account. It is the right rail for **large sums**, especially money destined for investment or property, because banks handle high values and create a clean record. It is usually the slowest (often one to a few business days) and its cost is a fee plus the bank's exchange margin plus, sometimes, correspondent-bank deductions along the way, the same anatomy as any [TT covered in the fees and FX guide](/blog/sme-transaction-fees-fx-spreads-kenya).

**Money transfer operators (MTOs).** The specialists: names like Western Union, MoneyGram, WorldRemit, Remitly, Sendwave, Wise, and Ria. They are built for **retail remittances**, are usually faster and cheaper than a bank wire for everyday amounts, and can deliver to a bank account, for cash pickup, or straight to a mobile wallet. Their cost is a visible fee plus an exchange margin, and the margin varies widely between them, which is the whole reason to compare.

**M-Pesa Global and direct-to-wallet.** Money sent from abroad landing **directly in the recipient's M-Pesa**, through Safaricom's international partnerships and various MTOs that pay out to mobile money. This is the fastest and most convenient route for ordinary family support: the recipient gets a text and the money is instantly usable, no bank visit, no cash pickup. It carries the mobile-wallet limits, so it suits everyday sums rather than a house deposit.

The one-line map: **bank wire for large and investment money, an MTO for everyday remittances, and M-Pesa Global for instant family support.** Which is cheapest for a given transfer is a question of comparing the shillings landed, not assuming.

### Part 2: The Real Cost, Worked

The cost of sending money home has two parts, and only one of them is advertised.

$$
\text{Total cost} = \text{Visible fee} + \underbrace{\left(\text{Interbank rate} - \text{Rate you were given}\right) \times \text{Amount}}_{\text{the exchange-rate margin, usually the bigger part}}
$$

Work a simple comparison. A relative wants to send the equivalent of **US$1,000** home. Take two routes, at an illustrative interbank rate of KES 129 to the dollar (rates move constantly; this is only to show the mechanics):

| | Route A: "low fee" | Route B: "fair rate" |
|---|---|---|
| Advertised fee | $4 | $9 |
| Exchange rate given | KES 125.1 (3% margin) | KES 127.7 (1% margin) |
| Shillings from the $996 / $991 converted | ~124,600 | ~126,560 |
| **Shillings actually received** | **~124,600** | **~126,560** |

Route A looks cheaper, a $4 fee against $9, and it is the more expensive one, because its poor exchange rate quietly took about 3% of the whole amount while Route B's fair rate took about 1%. The recipient gets roughly **KES 2,000 more** from the "pricier" route on a single $1,000 transfer. Multiply that by a monthly transfer over a year and the gap is a meaningful sum that simply stayed with the cheap-looking provider.

The discipline is exactly the one from [the fees and FX-spread guide](/blog/sme-transaction-fees-fx-spreads-kenya): **ask what rate you are being given and compare it to the real interbank rate, then compare providers on the final shillings received.** Several comparison tools and the providers' own quotes will show you the amount that lands before you send, use them, and let the providers compete for the transfer.

### Part 3: Where the Money Should Land

Choosing the destination is a separate decision from choosing the channel, and it matters.

- **M-Pesa** is best for **small, frequent, or urgent** support: rent, fees, an emergency. It is instant and immediately spendable, and it needs no bank visit. Its limits make it unsuitable for very large sums.
- **A bank account** is best for **large amounts and money with a purpose**: a property purchase, a business injection, or savings and investment. It handles high values, and it creates the **record** that matters if the money is later invested, taxed, or used to show source of funds.
- **Cash pickup** still exists and is occasionally necessary, but it is the least convenient and often not the cheapest; prefer an account or wallet where possible.

A useful rule for diaspora senders in particular: **money you intend to invest should arrive in a bank account, not a wallet.** Investments in [government securities via DhowCSD](/blog/cbk-diaspora-bond-access), a [property purchase](/blog/diaspora-land-purchase-kenya), or [offshore-linked assets](/blog/foreign-etfs-offshore-investing-kenya) all settle from and to a bank account, and a clean banking trail is what proves the money is yours and legitimately sourced.

### Part 4: Speed and Limits

Match the channel to how fast the money is needed and how large it is.

- **Instant** (minutes): most M-Pesa Global and mobile-wallet transfers, and many MTOs to a wallet.
- **Same day to a few days**: MTOs to a bank account, and bank telegraphic transfers, depending on the corridor and the banks.
- **Limits** rise as you move from wallet to bank: mobile money caps everyday transfers, while a bank account can receive large sums, subject to the receiving bank's own checks on large or unusual inflows.

For an emergency, an instant wallet transfer is worth a slightly worse rate. For a house deposit that is needed next week, a bank wire's better handling of a large sum matters more than shaving a day. Decide by urgency and size together, not by habit.

### Part 5: Is Money From Abroad Taxed?

This is the question every receiving family worries about, and the general position, as at 2026, is reassuring, with one important distinction.

- **Personal remittances and gifts from family are generally not taxable income** in Kenya. Money a relative sends to support the household is not treated as your earnings, so it is not income tax the receiver owes.
- **But money that is actually payment for work or business is income**, wherever it comes from. If you are a freelancer or business being paid by an overseas client and the money happens to arrive through a remittance channel, that is business income and is taxable in the normal way, the same as any other earnings, and it interacts with your [PAYE and freelance-tax position](/blog/paye-withholding-freelance-tax-kenya) and VAT where relevant.

The line, then, is *why* the money was sent, not *how* it arrived. Genuine family support is not taxed; earnings dressed as a remittance are still earnings. Tax rules change with each Finance Act and individual situations differ, so confirm your position with the [KRA](https://www.kra.go.ke/) or a tax agent, especially if you receive regular large inflows or are paid from abroad for services. The safe practice for anyone receiving business income from overseas is to keep clean records of what each inflow was for.

### Part 6: Scams That Target the Diaspora and Their Families

Money crossing borders attracts fraud aimed at both ends of the transfer, and a few patterns recur.

- **Advance-fee and "release fee" cons.** The recipient is told a large transfer is waiting but a fee, tax, or clearance charge must be paid first to release it. There is no transfer; the fee is the scam. No legitimate remittance requires the recipient to pay to receive.
- **Fake investment and land deals aimed at the diaspora.** Senders abroad, keen to invest at home and unable to inspect in person, are prime targets for fraudulent land and "high-return" schemes. The defences are in [buying land from the diaspora without getting burned](/blog/diaspora-land-purchase-kenya) and the neutral tests in [how to evaluate any investment opportunity](/blog/evaluate-investment-opportunity-kenya); the short version is that unverifiable urgency is the red flag.
- **Romance and impersonation scams** that persuade a diaspora sender to remit to a stranger, or persuade a family member at home that a relative abroad urgently needs money sent on.
- **Wrong-send and reversal cons** on the receiving M-Pesa, exactly as covered in [the fraud and reversals guide](/blog/recover-wrong-payment-fraud-kenya): unexpected money plus a plea to forward it on.

The through-line: **legitimate money coming to you never requires you to send money first**, and any pressure to act urgently, pay a release fee, or invest before you can verify is the tell. Slow down and confirm through a channel you trust.

### Risk Factors

| Risk | How it arises | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Judging by the fee | Ignoring the exchange rate | Losing more to a poor rate than a higher fee would have cost |
| Wrong destination | Large sums into a wallet, support into slow channels | Hitting limits, or paying for speed you did not need |
| No record for investment | Money to invest landing informally | Cannot prove source of funds when it matters |
| Informal channels | Sending outside licensed providers | No recourse if the money is lost or delayed |
| Taxing a gift, or hiding income | Confusing support with earnings | Overpaying, or a compliance problem on overseas income |
| Release-fee scam | Paying to "unlock" a transfer | Money lost to a transfer that never existed |
| Diaspora investment fraud | Buying unseen on urgency | Losing capital sent home to invest |

### Decision Framework: Sending or Receiving From Abroad

**How many shillings actually land?** Compare providers on the final received amount, not the advertised fee. The rate is where the cost hides.

**Where should it arrive?** Wallet for small and urgent; bank account for large sums and anything to be invested or that needs a record.

**How fast and how large?** Match the channel to urgency and size together. Pay for speed only when you need it.

**Is this a gift or income?** Family support is generally not taxed; overseas earnings are. Keep records and confirm with KRA if you receive business income from abroad.

**Is anyone asking me to pay or send first?** Then it is almost certainly a scam. Legitimate incoming money never requires an upfront payment from the receiver.

### Bengula View

Three observations from the desk.

First, **the exchange-rate margin is the most successful hidden fee in consumer finance, and remittances are where it does the most quiet damage.** Families comparing "send for $3" against "send for $8" routinely pick the one that costs them more, because the cheaper-looking option clawed back its discount and then some inside the rate. The fix is a single habit: look at the shillings received, not the fee. Every serious provider will show you that number before you send, so the information is free; people just do not ask for it.

Second, **destination is a decision, not a default.** M-Pesa's convenience is so great that money which should go to a bank account, a deposit, an investment, a large sum, gets sent to a wallet out of habit, then hits limits or leaves no usable record. Decide by purpose: instant support to the wallet, serious money to the bank. For diaspora investors especially, the clean bank trail is not bureaucracy, it is what lets you buy a bond, a plot, or a fund without a source-of-funds problem later.

Third, **the diaspora is targeted precisely because distance prevents verification, and the antidote is to refuse to be rushed.** Almost every scam aimed at senders abroad or their families relies on urgency and the inability to check in person, a plot selling today, a fee to release a transfer now, a relative in sudden trouble. Real opportunities and real transfers survive a pause. Build in the pause, verify through someone you trust on the ground, and the great majority of these losses simply do not happen.

### Conclusion

Money flows home to Kenya in enormous volume, and how much of each transfer arrives depends far less on the advertised fee than on the exchange rate hidden inside it. Compare the ways in, bank wire, money transfer operator, M-Pesa Global, on the shillings that actually land, send everyday support to the wallet and serious money to the bank, and use licensed channels that give you recourse. Understand that genuine family support is not taxed while overseas earnings are, and that any request to pay or send money first is the signature of a scam.

Do that, and the billions that come home each year work harder: more shillings reach the family, the investment money arrives with a clean trail, and the fraud that preys on distance finds no purchase. The transfer is easy; making it land well is the skill, and it is entirely learnable.
