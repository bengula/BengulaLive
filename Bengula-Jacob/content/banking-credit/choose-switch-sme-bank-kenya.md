Ask an SME owner why they bank where they bank and you usually get a version of the same answer: it was where they had a personal account, or the branch was close, or somebody at the chama recommended it, eleven years ago.

That is a strange way to choose a supplier you pay every month, who holds all your money, who decides whether you get credit, and whose systems dictate how fast you get paid. If a raw material supplier were chosen that way and never reviewed, most owners would recognise the problem immediately. With banks, the review simply never happens, partly because switching feels frightening and partly because nobody has ever explained how to do it without breaking something.

This guide is about doing it deliberately. It gives you a **scorecard** for judging a bank on the seven things that actually matter to a business, a framework for when running **two banks** is genuinely rational, a **switch sequence** that protects your facilities and your collections, and the **red flags** that mean you should have moved already. What it deliberately does not do is re-list Kenyan banking products, because [the banking guide](/blog/ultimate-guide-to-banking-in-kenya) already maps those.

> **Key Insight:** You are not choosing an institution, you are choosing a **relationship, a tariff and a set of rails**, and those three things vary enormously between banks that look identical from outside. The rails determine how fast money reaches you. The tariff determines how much of it survives. The relationship determines whether you get credit in the month you need it. Brand, branch network and advertising determine almost nothing.

### Part 1: The Scorecard

Seven criteria. Score each from 1 to 5 for your current bank, then for any bank you are considering, and weight them for your own business rather than in the abstract.

**1. Relationship manager quality and responsiveness.** This is the criterion owners under-weight the most and regret the most. A good RM knows your business, tells you what the credit committee will ask before you submit, warns you when something is going to be declined, and answers the phone in the week you need an answer rather than the month. A bad one is a contact detail on an email footer. The difference shows up precisely when you have a live opportunity and a two-week window, which is the argument [the RM as a growth asset piece](/blog/why-rm-is-sme-growth-asset) makes at length. **Test it before you commit:** ask a moderately complex question and see how long the answer takes and whether it is right.

**2. Tariff, in full and in writing.** Not the headline monthly fee. The full schedule: transfers by rail, cash deposit and withdrawal charges, cheque handling, statement fees, standing orders, card fees, ledger fees, and the charges that only appear when you do something unusual. [The transaction fees and FX spreads guide](/blog/sme-transaction-fees-fx-spreads-kenya) shows how to run a 30-day fee audit on your own statements, and that audit is the single most useful preparation for this conversation, because it converts an abstract comparison into your actual annual cost.

**3. Foreign exchange pricing.** If you import, export or hold any foreign currency, this is often larger than every other fee combined and it is almost never quoted. The spread between the rate you get and the interbank rate is the real price, and it is negotiable at volume in a way most owners never discover because they never ask. The anatomy of that spread is in the same fees guide.

**4. Trade and lending capability.** Does the bank actually have a trade desk that can issue a letter of credit, a guarantee or an invoice discounting line, and does it understand your sector? A bank that technically offers these products but processes them slowly and reluctantly is not offering them in any useful sense. If your growth path runs through [trade finance](/blog/trade-finance-kenya-complete-guide), [LPO finance](/blog/lpo-purchase-order-finance-kenya) or [bank guarantees](/blog/bank-guarantees-kenya-sme-guide), this criterion may outrank everything else on the list.

**5. Digital rails and integration.** Can you do bulk payments properly, with maker-checker approval? Does the internet banking platform work reliably? Does it integrate with your accounting system, and can you get clean statement exports? Does it connect to your collections, your Pay Bill, your card acquiring? The operational cost of a bad platform is paid every single week in staff time. [Bulk payments and payroll](/blog/bulk-payments-payroll-payouts-kenya) covers what good looks like here.

**6. Collections fit.** How does your money actually arrive? If most revenue comes through M-Pesa, the settlement arrangement into your bank account and its timing matter more than anything else on this list, as [the M-Pesa for business guide](/blog/mpesa-for-business-paybill-till-kenya) sets out. If you take cards, the acquiring relationship and settlement period matter, per [the card payments guide](/blog/card-payments-pos-kenya-business). If customers pay by transfer, what you care about is which rails the bank supports and at what cost, which is [the payment rails guide](/blog/how-to-move-money-kenya-payment-rails).

**7. Multi-currency and branch reality.** Can you hold foreign currency accounts if you need them? And, honestly assessed, how often do you actually need a branch? Many owners still weight branch proximity heavily and then visit twice a year. Score what you do, not what you imagine you do.

**Weighting.** A retailer collecting through a till weights collections fit and tariff heavily and trade capability at zero. An importer weights FX and trade desk above everything. A services business with twelve staff weights digital rails and RM quality. **The scorecard is only useful once you have weighted it for the business you actually run.**

### Part 2: When Two Banks Is the Right Answer

Running more than one banking relationship is often treated as disorganisation. For a business past a certain size it is deliberate structure, and there are four sound reasons for it.

**Redundancy.** Systems fail, accounts get frozen during a compliance review, a platform goes down on the day payroll runs. A second working relationship with a funded account turns an existential problem into an inconvenience. This alone justifies it for any business that cannot miss a payment.

**Rate and price competition.** A bank that knows it is your only option prices accordingly. A bank that knows there is a live alternative behaves differently, on lending margins, on FX spreads, and on fee waivers. The existence of the second relationship is worth money even when you do not use it much.

**Different capabilities.** One bank may have an excellent trade desk and a mediocre digital platform. Another the reverse. Splitting by capability rather than loyalty is rational.

**Separating collections from facilities.** Where a lender has a charge over your operating account, all your incoming revenue is visible and reachable. Some businesses sensibly keep a collections account elsewhere. This one comes with caveats and should be discussed openly with your lender rather than done quietly, because concealing revenue from a bank that has lent against it damages the relationship you will need later.

**The costs of multi-banking are real too**, and they are why you should not do it too early: two sets of fees and minimums, two reconciliations, split transaction history that weakens the credit picture at both banks, and more administrative surface for fraud and error. For a business below roughly the size where it has a dedicated finance person, one bank done well usually beats two done casually.

**The practical structure that works:** a **primary bank** that holds the operating account, the facilities and the depth of relationship, and a **secondary bank** with a live, funded, occasionally used account, enough history to be credible, and a named contact. Do not let the secondary relationship go dormant, because a dormant account is not redundancy.

### Part 3: The Switch Sequence

This is the part that stops people, and the fear is legitimate: a badly sequenced switch breaks payroll, bounces supplier payments, or calls in a facility. Done in the right order, it is tedious rather than dangerous.

The governing rule is simple: **never close anything until its replacement is live and tested.** There is a well-worn failure mode where an owner, frustrated with their bank, closes the account first and spends the next six weeks in a hole. The sequence below exists to avoid that.

1. **Run the fee audit first.** Thirty days of your own statements, every charge categorised. Without this you are negotiating on feeling. With it, you may find the incumbent will match rather than lose you, which is the cheapest possible outcome.
2. **Open the new account and leave everything else alone.** Nothing moves yet. Get the account opened, the mandates right, the signatories and approval limits configured, and the digital platform working. [The business banking journey guide](/blog/new-business-banking-journey-kenya) covers the setup detail.
3. **Test the new rails with small real transactions.** Send a payment on each rail you use. Receive one. Run a trial bulk file. Export a statement into your accounting system. Find the problems now, while they are harmless.
4. **Negotiate the facilities before you move the flows, not after.** This is the sequencing point owners most often get wrong. Your negotiating position is strongest while you still hold the relationship and the turnover the new bank wants. Get the facility offers in writing, with pricing, while you are still a prospect.
5. **Move collections deliberately and in stages.** Redirect customers, update the settlement account for your Pay Bill or till, and update card acquiring. Do this in waves, watching each one land, rather than all at once. Expect a tail of customers who keep paying into the old account for months, which is one reason the old account stays open.
6. **Move outgoing payments.** Standing orders, direct debits, supplier payment templates, payroll, statutory payments, subscriptions. Build a written checklist of every recurring payment from the last twelve months of statements; memory will miss several.
7. **Only now refinance any facilities**, once the new bank has seen real turnover through the new account and the offers are firm. Repay and release the old facility against the new one, coordinated so there is never a gap in either direction.
8. **Run both accounts in parallel for at least three months.** Keep the old one funded enough to absorb stragglers and anything you forgot. This overlap is the insurance premium on the whole exercise and it is cheap.
9. **Close the old account properly.** Get written confirmation of closure and a final statement. Make sure no facility, guarantee, charge or security remains registered against you at the old bank, and that any security is formally discharged.

> **Risk Factors:** The specific things that go wrong are: closing before the new facilities are approved, so you lose working capital mid-switch; forgetting a recurring payment and defaulting on something that reaches a credit bureau, which [the CRB guide](/blog/how-to-fix-your-crb-listing-kenya) explains how to unwind; leaving a security or charge undischarged at the old bank, which surfaces years later during a refinance; moving collections faster than customers can update, so revenue lands in an account you have closed; and switching to escape a problem that is actually about your own numbers, because a weak balance sheet looks the same to every bank.

### Part 4: Red Flags That Justify Leaving

Some dissatisfactions are worth a conversation. These are worth a move.

**Nobody owns your relationship.** You have had four RMs in two years, or none, and every request starts from zero. This is the most common and most damaging.

**Credit decisions are slow and unexplained.** Not declined, which is a legitimate answer, but silent for weeks and then declined with no reason you can act on. A bank that will not tell you why cannot be improved on next time.

**Your tariff has drifted and nobody told you.** Fees that appeared without notice, or that no longer match what you were quoted. The fee audit finds these.

**FX pricing you cannot get in writing.** If the desk will not quote you a spread against a reference rate, you are being priced on ignorance.

**The digital platform costs you staff hours every week.** A platform that fails during payment runs is a direct operating cost, and it compounds.

**Settlement timing that damages your cash cycle.** Money that takes days longer to reach you than it should is working capital you are lending to your bank for free, which is exactly the problem [the working capital cycle guide](/blog/working-capital-cycle-kenya-smes) and [the 13-week forecast](/blog/13-week-cash-forecast-kenya-sme) are built around.

**A product mismatch you have outgrown.** The bank was right for a sole trader with one account and is wrong for a company with fifteen staff, imports and a trade line. That is not a failure by anyone; it is a signal to move.

And one honest counter-flag. **If several banks decline you, the problem is probably not the bank.** Switching will not fix a weak set of numbers, an unexplained decline in turnover or a thin credit history. In that case the work is on the proposal and the fundamentals, which is what [the bank proposal guide](/blog/anatomy-of-a-bank-proposal-kenya) and [the financial ratios guide](/blog/sme-financial-ratios-kenya) exist for. Moving banks to escape your own balance sheet just resets the clock on the same conversation.

### Decision Framework: Should You Move

**Have you run the fee audit?** If not, do that before anything else. It either produces a number that justifies the move or a lever that fixes the current relationship.

**Is the problem the bank or the business?** If your numbers are the issue, fix those first. A switch will not help and will cost you your history.

**Which two scorecard criteria actually matter for you?** Weight them properly. Most businesses find the decision turns on two of the seven, not all of them.

**Do you have facilities in place?** If yes, the sequence matters enormously and the facilities must be negotiated before the flows move.

**Could a second bank solve this instead of a switch?** Often the answer is redundancy plus competitive pressure, not a wholesale move.

**Can you tolerate three months of parallel running?** If not, you are not ready to start. That overlap is not optional.

### Bengula View

Three observations from the desk.

First, **the relationship manager is worth more than the tariff for most SMEs, and it is priced at zero in every comparison.** Owners will spend weeks comparing monthly fees that differ by a few hundred shillings and then accept a bank where nobody knows their name, and the cost of that shows up exactly once, in the month they need a facility quickly and there is nobody to advocate for the file. A responsive RM who understands your sector shortens every credit decision you will ever make. Test for that before you test for anything else.

Second, **the threat of leaving is worth more than leaving, and almost nobody uses it.** A business that arrives with a 30-day fee audit, a clear statement of what it needs, and a credible alternative usually gets a materially better outcome from its existing bank than one that simply moves. Banks price on relationship risk, and a customer who has visibly done the work is a different kind of customer. Do the audit even if you intend to stay; it is the cheapest negotiation you will ever run.

Third, **the switch fails at step four, every time.** Owners move their collections and their payments first, because those feel like the switch, and then discover they are negotiating facilities from a position of no turnover at the new bank and no leverage at the old one. Get the credit conversation done while you still have something to offer. Move the money afterwards. That single ordering decision is the difference between a switch that improves your terms and one that quietly costs you your overdraft.

### Conclusion

Your bank is a supplier, and it is probably the least reviewed supplier relationship you have. Score it on the seven things that affect your business: the relationship manager, the full tariff, FX pricing, trade and lending capability, digital rails, collections fit, and the multi-currency and branch reality. Weight those for the business you actually run rather than the one in the brochure.

If the score is poor, the answer may be a second bank rather than a switch, because redundancy and competitive pressure fix a surprising share of the complaints. If it is a switch, run it in order: audit, open, test, **negotiate the facilities**, then move collections, then payments, then refinance, then overlap for three months, then close properly with every security discharged.

And before any of it, be honest about whether the problem is the bank or the numbers. One of those a switch can fix.

### Frequently Asked Questions

**How long does switching business banks take?** Plan for three to six months end to end, most of which is the deliberate overlap while collections migrate and stragglers keep paying into the old account. The account opening itself is the fastest part.

**Will switching hurt my credit standing?** Not in itself. What hurts is a missed recurring payment during a badly sequenced switch, or closing a facility and leaving a gap. Build the recurring-payment checklist from twelve months of statements.

**Should I tell my current bank I am looking?** Usually yes, after you have done the fee audit. A specific, evidenced conversation often produces better terms, and it costs you nothing to have it before you commit.

**Is it bad to have accounts at two banks?** No, past a certain size it is good practice, for redundancy, price competition and capability. Below that size the double administration usually outweighs the benefit.

**My bank keeps declining my facility. Should I move?** Find out why first. If several banks would decline you on the same numbers, the work is on the proposal and the fundamentals, not on the institution.

**What do I need to check before closing the old account?** Written confirmation of closure, a final statement, and confirmation that no facility, guarantee or registered security remains outstanding or undischarged in your name.
